The Shape of the Argument

The Epilogue functions as both a recapitulation and a challenge. Thelen and Smith step back from the empirical detail of the preceding eleven chapters to survey the argument they have built and to state, with considerable frankness, what remains undone. The Epilogue is brief relative to the chapters, but it performs essential rhetorical work: it forces the reader to see the individual case studies (stepping, reaching, categorization, A-not-B) not as isolated demonstrations of dynamic systems principles but as convergent evidence for a unified theory of developmental process. Each case study was selected because it allowed the authors to falsify a specific claim of the dominant frameworks: stepping falsified maturational timetables, reaching falsified modular programming accounts, categorization falsified innate concept structures, and A-not-B falsified stage theory. The Epilogue asks the reader to recognize that these are not four separate arguments but a single argument made four times, each time from a different empirical angle.

What the Book Claims to Have Shown

The Epilogue consolidates the book’s central achievements into a compact set of propositions. First, that development is not the progressive unveiling of pre-formed structures, whether genetic, maturational, or innate-cognitive, but the emergent product of multiple interacting components, none of which contains the developmental plan. Second, that the appropriate unit of analysis for developmental science is not the organism, the brain, or the environment, but the organism-environment system as a coupled dynamical entity. Third, that the appearance of discontinuity in development (stages, milestones, qualitative shifts) can be explained by the mathematics of nonlinear dynamics without positing discontinuity in the underlying process. Phase transitions produce sharp behavioral change from smooth parameter change, and this resolves the apparent paradox of how continuous biological processes can yield discrete behavioral stages.

Fourth, and most methodologically consequential, the Epilogue reiterates that the way development has been studied is itself part of the problem. Cross-sectional designs, group averages, and static task administrations are not merely imprecise. They are systematically misleading, because they filter out exactly the information that dynamic systems theory identifies as most diagnostic: within-subject variability, real-time behavioral trajectories, transition dynamics, and context-sensitivity. The Epilogue does not frame this as a polite methodological suggestion. It frames it as an indictment. The methods that developmental psychology has relied on cannot, in principle, reveal developmental process, because they were designed to characterize developmental products.

The Unfinished Business

Thelen and Smith are notably candid about the limitations of their own framework as of 1994. They acknowledge that the dynamic systems approach has been most successful in domains where behavior can be described in continuous, quantifiable terms: limb kinematics, stepping frequency, reaching trajectories. The extension to higher cognition, language, and social understanding, while conceptually motivated, remains largely promissory. They do not pretend that the Watt governor analogy constitutes a theory of symbolic thought. They present it as an existence proof that intelligent regulation does not require representation, and as a provocation to the field to take seriously the possibility that the gap between sensorimotor and symbolic cognition is smaller and different in kind than has been assumed.

They also acknowledge the computational challenge. The dynamic field models sketched in the reaching and A-not-B chapters are qualitative or semi-quantitative. A fully specified dynamic systems account of any developmental phenomenon requires formal models with identifiable parameters, testable predictions about individual trajectories, and quantitative fits to real behavioral data. This work was underway in 1994 and would accelerate dramatically in the following decade, particularly through the contributions of Spencer, Schöner, and their collaborators, who would turn the A-not-B dynamic field model into a precise, computationally implemented theory with quantitative predictions about the effects of delay, number of A trials, and spatial separation between targets. But at the time of the Epilogue, this formal machinery was still in its early stages.

The Challenge to the Field

The Epilogue’s most pointed passages concern the institutional and sociological barriers to adopting the dynamic systems framework. Thelen and Smith observe that the framework requires skills that developmental psychologists are not trained in: nonlinear dynamics, differential equations, time-series analysis, computational modeling. It requires a tolerance for complexity and individual differences that runs against the grain of psychology’s preference for clean main effects and replicable group-level patterns. And it requires a willingness to spend years tracking individual participants through developmental transitions, a research strategy that is poorly rewarded by publication norms and tenure clocks that favor novelty and volume over depth.

These observations are not complaints. They are a diagnosis of why the field has been slow to adopt methods that, in the authors’ view, are better matched to the phenomena under study. The Epilogue implies, without quite stating, that developmental psychology’s theoretical commitments (to stages, to competence-performance distinctions, to unitary constructs like object permanence) are partially maintained by the methods the field uses, which in turn are maintained by the training and incentive structures of academic psychology. Changing the theory requires changing the methods, but changing the methods requires changing the training, and changing the training requires that the theory be taken seriously enough to justify the investment. The Epilogue identifies this circularity without pretending to resolve it.

Legacy and Trajectory

Read thirty years after publication, the Epilogue has a prophetic quality. The dynamic field theory of spatial cognition, growing directly from the A-not-B work, became one of the most formally precise theories in developmental cognitive science. The emphasis on variability as informative rather than noise has been absorbed into developmental methodology more broadly. The insistence on embodiment as constitutive of cognition anticipated the embodied cognition movement that reshaped cognitive science in the 2000s. At the same time, the broader aspirations remain only partially realized. The dynamic systems approach has not replaced stage theory or nativism as the dominant framework. It exists alongside them, sometimes as complement, sometimes as competitor, and sometimes as a methodological toolkit borrowed by researchers whose theoretical commitments lie elsewhere. The call for fundamental methodological reorientation has been heard but not fully heeded.

The Epilogue closes, fittingly, not with a summary but with an invitation. The dynamic systems framework is presented as a research program, not a finished theory. Its value lies not in having answered the major questions of developmental science but in having shown that the major questions were being asked in the wrong way. The phenomena are real. The methods are available. The theoretical vocabulary is precise enough to generate testable predictions and flexible enough to accommodate new empirical discoveries. What the framework asks of the field is a willingness to begin again: to look at development not as a series of achievements on a timetable but as a continuous, context-dependent, multiply-caused process of self-organization that is as complex, as beautiful, and as irreducibly dynamic as the infants it seeks to understand.
