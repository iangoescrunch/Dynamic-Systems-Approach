Chapter 9 applies the full NDS apparatus to a single empirical case: learning to reach. The contribution is not a motor development study decorated with dynamic vocabulary. It is a demonstration of how NDS assumptions dictate the research design, from the selection of collective variables and the identification of control parameters to the consolidation of measures across nested timescales. The reaching data test whether the causal claims developed across the preceding chapters hold when examined at a resolution fine enough to expose the process.

The Inadequacy of Existing Accounts

Thelen opens by eliminating two rival frameworks. Nativist accounts (Bower, 1989; Trevarthen, 1984) treat reaching as prefigured knowledge uniting vision and prehension. This position collapses under scrutiny. Newborn arm extensions occur only when paced and constrained by the ability to produce and control movement, lack visual guidance, and show no continuity with later reaching; the behavior nearly disappears for weeks before functional reaches emerge (Hofsten, 1984). If reaching is encoded in the genome, what specifies the translation of the code into a coordinated limb trajectory?

Constructionist accounts (Piaget, 1952; Bruner, 1973) propose that reaching is built from progressive mapping of the seen hand and target. The constructionist theory depends on infants first visually localizing the object and hand, comparing the two, and directing movement on the basis of that visual assessment. If this sequence is necessary, infants should perform worse in the absence of vision. Yet Clifton, Muir, Ashmead, and Clarkson (1993) showed that, at first, infants reached for onset-contacted objects equally well in the dark and for sounding objects they could not see. Piaget observed hand-object regard only after infants had already moved their hands near the target. The evidence for a look-move-compare construction sequence is weak.

Designing Research Under NDS Assumptions

The Thelen et al. (1993) longitudinal study of four infants (Nathan, Gabriel, Justin, Hannah) was designed to satisfy four core requirements that follow directly from the conceptual model. First, the study maps intrinsic dynamics: the attractor landscape each infant occupies before the transition to reaching, expressed through real-time behavioral trajectories that reveal preferred states and their stability. Second, it integrates timescales by recording both the real-time structure of individual reaches and the developmental-time evolution of reaching patterns across weeks and months. Third, it searches for control parameters across multiple levels (kinematics, kinetics, muscle activation, posture), recognizing that the rate-limiting component is unknown in advance and could reside at any level. Fourth, it seeks an empirically derived collective variable that condenses the system's degrees of freedom into a sensitive index of change (pp. 250-251, Thelen & Smith, 1994).

This design is itself the argument. The study measures behavior at multiple levels, not because more data is better, but because the core assumption that reaching is soft assembled from equivalent, interacting components requires multi-level observation. The weekly longitudinal schedule is calibrated to the timescale of change, so that transitions can be captured rather than averaged over.

Gabriel and Hannah: Different Problems, Common Dynamics

The Gabriel-Hannah contrast is the chapter's sharpest empirical argument against single-cause explanation. Gabriel, a motorically active infant who reached at 15 weeks, produced vigorous bilateral flapping movements whose phase-plane portraits traced smooth ellipses characteristic of a limit-cycle attractor (p. 255). His arms behaved like periodically forced springs. The task for Gabriel was to disrupt this cycle attractor and convert it to a point attractor whose endpoint fell near the toy. He accomplished this through massive co-contraction of shoulder and upper arm muscles, which stiffened the limb, damped the high-velocity oscillations, and allowed a controlled approach. His first reaches were embedded in his ongoing flapping movements and continuous with them.

Hannah, a quiet infant who reached at 22 weeks, faced an inverse problem. Her spontaneous movements were slow and low amplitude, with her hands resting on her chest or in her mouth. She needed to generate sufficient force to lift her arm against gravity and extend her naturally flexed elbow. Her first reaches were smooth, slow, and more adult-like in trajectory than Gabriel's, but her muscle patterns showed the same co-contraction strategy (p. 260).

The theoretical point is precise. Gabriel and Hannah solved different biomechanical problems with individually discovered solutions, yet the underlying process was identical: each modulated the forces available in the existing system to shift the attractor from its current regime to one that brought the hand near the toy. Neither infant executed a pre-specified reaching program. Both exploited intrinsic dynamics by the same control mechanism (stiffness regulation) applied to opposite initial conditions. But can motivation alone produce a coordinated reach? Isolating any single factor in the causal chain causes it's collapse. This is distributed causality in action: the "cause" of reaching is the cooperative interaction of motivation, postural control, limb biomechanics, and force modulation throughout the task of contacting a seen object. No component is privileged.

Nathan: Exploration and Selection Across the First Year

Nathan's year-long data demonstrate how the same selectionist process operates at the developmental timescale. Hand speed at contact and directional accuracy serve as collective variables. Neither improved linearly. After reach onset at 15 weeks, Nathan explored a wide range of force-speed combinations. Through mid-year, performance plateaued with continued session-to-session variability. In the final months, both variables self-equilibrated into consistent, efficient patterns (pp. 272-273).

The variability pattern is the finding. It is not noise obscuring a monotonic learning curve. It is the exploration phase that a selectionist system requires before adaptive patterns can be retained. Nathan's muscle activation followed the same trajectory: initially variable and patternless, then intermittently showing co-activation, then stabilizing on a consistent co-activation pattern that, while not adult-like, represented a coordinated and functional solution (Spencer, Kamm, & Thelen, 1992).

Shifts in bimanual coordination provide evidence that the control parameter itself changes over developmental time. Nathan shifted from bimanual to unimanual reaching at week 20, coinciding with improved force control in the reaching hand and suppression of energetic overflow to the opposite limb. He returned to bimanual reaching in the final months, coinciding with the emergence of cooperative two-handed actions. The control parameter for the first transition (force differentiation between limbs) differs from that which later reorganized the system (intentional bimanual coordination). Single-cause accounts cannot track a system whose rate-limiting component changes as the system develops (p. 276).

Postural development co-varied with reaching phases. Head and torso control coincided with initial force modulation; hands-and-knees and sitting with the next plateau; crawling and walking with the final stabilization. Thelen is cautious: these are associations in one infant, not confirmed causal claims. But the co-variation is predicted by the framework, not by accounts that treat reaching as an isolated skill on its own timetable.

Action as Knowledge

The chapter concludes by dissolving the boundary between motor skill and cognition. Reaching is knowledge: it requires understanding of the body's dynamics, the spatial location of objects, and the relationship between force modulation and environmental outcomes. The activation patterns that constitute this knowledge create expectations, direct attention, and function dynamically in the same manner as patterns traditionally labeled cognitive (p. 277). The distinction between forming a motor category (appropriate stiffness controls for transporting the hand to a target) and forming a perceptual category (the properties of solid objects) is not logical but conventional. Both are products of the same perception-action dynamics operating in real time and across developmental time. The process that produced Gabriel's first reach, as well as the process that produces an infant's first demonstration of object permanence, share a common structure: exploration of the system's variability, selection of functional patterns, and progressive stabilization of those patterns into attractor basins whose depth reflects the history of the organism's activity.
