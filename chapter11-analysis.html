The Hardest Problem

Chapter 11 is where Thelen and Smith confront the objection they know is coming: even if the dynamic systems framework can explain infant reaching, stepping, and perseverative search, can it account for the kinds of cognition that seem most distant from the body? Language, abstract reasoning, planning, counterfactual thought. These are the capacities that have historically been used to justify the separation of cognition from action, the very separation that the entire book has been working to dissolve. If dynamic systems theory cannot at least sketch a path toward explaining symbolic thought, then the framework remains confined to a sophisticated but ultimately limited account of sensorimotor development.

Thelen and Smith do not claim to have solved this problem. What they provide is an argument for why the problem has been misframed, and a set of conceptual tools for approaching it differently. The misframing, they contend, consists in treating symbolic thought as a qualitatively different kind of process from perception-action coupling, one that requires a different theoretical vocabulary and different explanatory principles. The dynamic systems alternative is that symbolic thought is a compression and extension of the same embodied dynamics that produce reaching and walking, achieved through new forms of coupling (especially linguistic coupling with other agents) rather than through a transition to a fundamentally different computational architecture.

The Watt Governor Analogy

The chapter’s most striking conceptual move is the extended discussion of the Watt centrifugal governor, borrowed from the philosophy of mind literature but put to distinctly developmental use. The Watt governor is a mechanical device that regulates the speed of a steam engine. Two weighted arms are attached to the engine’s output shaft. As the engine speeds up, centrifugal force causes the arms to rise, which partially closes the steam valve, which reduces engine speed. As speed decreases, the arms fall, opening the valve, increasing speed. The result is stable regulation without any component that represents the desired speed, compares it to actual speed, or computes the corrective action.

Thelen and Smith use the governor to make a point about the nature of intelligence itself. The standard cognitive science approach would model the engine-regulation problem by positing an internal representation of the target speed, a comparator that detects discrepancies, and a controller that issues correction commands. The governor achieves the same functional outcome with no representations, no comparisons, and no commands. The intelligence is in the coupling between components, not in any component’s internal processing. The developmental implication is direct: if something as functionally intelligent as speed regulation can be achieved through coupling dynamics alone, then the burden of proof shifts to those who claim that infant behavior requires internal representations, innate knowledge structures, or central executive controllers. Perhaps what looks like representation in the infant is better understood as a stable pattern of coupled perception-action-memory dynamics, just as the governor’s regulation is a coupled mechanical dynamic rather than an implicit representation of the target speed.

Motivation and the Energetic Landscape

A significant and underappreciated section of the chapter addresses motivation, a topic conspicuously absent from most cognitive developmental theory. Piaget’s framework has no principled place for motivation; the infant progresses through stages because the organism’s inherent tendency toward equilibration drives the assimilation-accommodation cycle. Nativist accounts have even less to say: if knowledge is innate, the question of why the infant bothers to explore the world at all becomes puzzling.

Thelen and Smith incorporate motivation not as a separate system that triggers or modulates cognition from outside, but as a dimension of the dynamical landscape itself. A highly motivated infant has a deeper attractor basin around the goal state, meaning that a wider range of initial conditions and perturbations will still result in goal-directed behavior. A less motivated infant has a shallower basin, making the behavior more susceptible to disruption by competing attractors. Critically, this is not a metaphor. The energetic parameters of the infant’s body, arousal level, metabolic state, and emotional valence, directly modulate the dynamics of the perception-action system. An infant who is hungry, tired, or upset produces qualitatively different reaching dynamics than the same infant in an alert state, not because the infant “knows less” about objects when tired but because the dynamical landscape has been reshaped by the infant’s energetic condition.

This treatment of motivation dissolves the cognition-emotion boundary in the same way that earlier chapters dissolved the cognition-action boundary. There is no point in the system where “cold” cognitive processing hands off to “hot” motivational processing. The temperature, so to speak, is a property of the entire dynamical landscape at every moment.

Toward Symbolic Thought

The chapter’s treatment of symbolic thought is necessarily more speculative, but it outlines a coherent research program. The key move is to argue that language does not require a break from embodied dynamics but introduces a new coupling medium. Before language, the infant’s cognitive dynamics are coupled to the physical environment through perception and action. Language adds a new channel: the infant’s dynamics become coupled to the dynamics of other agents through shared symbols. This coupling does not replace perception-action coupling; it supplements it with a medium that allows coordination across spatial and temporal gaps. You can talk about absent objects, past events, and future actions not because language gives you access to a disembodied representational system but because linguistic coupling extends the temporal and spatial reach of the same dynamic coordination processes that were previously limited to the here-and-now.

Thelen and Smith draw on Edelman’s notion of higher-order consciousness to flesh out this account. Primary consciousness arises from reentrant mapping between perceptual categorization and value-category memory. Higher-order consciousness adds a new reentrant loop connecting ongoing perception to a symbolic (linguistic) memory system, allowing the organism to model itself as an agent embedded in time. The dynamic systems contribution is to insist that this additional loop does not operate on a different computational principle. It is the same reentrant, self-organizing, context-dependent dynamic, operating over a richer and more abstract coupling structure.

The Continuity Thesis

The chapter concludes by restating the book’s most radical claim in its strongest form: there is no point in development at which one kind of process gives way to another. The same principles of self-organization, exploration, selective stabilization, and context-dependent soft assembly that explain newborn stepping also operate in adult planning and abstract reasoning. What changes across development is not the nature of the process but the complexity of the coupling structure: more components, more timescales, more coupling channels, more deeply nested attractors. The continuity thesis does not flatten the distinction between infant and adult cognition. It insists that the distinction is one of dynamic complexity, not of computational kind.

Thelen and Smith are transparent about the fact that this claim remains largely programmatic at the level of adult cognition. But the chapter makes a persuasive case that the alternative, positing a qualitative shift from dynamic-embodied to symbolic-representational processing at some developmental point, creates more problems than it solves. It requires identifying when the shift happens, explaining what triggers it, and accounting for the abundant evidence that adult cognition remains deeply context-dependent, embodied, and dynamically assembled. The dynamic systems approach avoids these problems by treating the apparent gap between sensorimotor and symbolic cognition as a difference in coupling structure rather than a difference in computational kind.
