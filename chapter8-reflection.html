The Recumbent Bicycle and the Posture-Specificity of Knowledge

I can ride a bicycle. This seems like a stable, generalizable piece of knowledge, the kind of thing people invoke when they say “it’s like riding a bike.” I tried riding a recumbent bicycle once and kept crashing. Lying down changed my accessibility to the physics of riding a bike without falling over.

On an upright bicycle, balance is a continuous perception-action loop: vestibular input specifies tilt, proprioception from legs and core specifies posture relative to the frame, and the rider compensates through weight shifts, handlebar corrections, and pedal torque. These signals are coupled to a specific body geometry where the center of gravity sits above the contact patch and can be shifted laterally through hip, shoulder, and knee adjustments. The recumbent eliminated most of this repertoire. My body was fixed from the shoulders down, reclined with legs extended forward, center of gravity low and locked in place. The steering operated through an indirect mechanical linkage. Every perception-action coupling I relied on for upright balance was either absent or producing signals that no longer mapped to the corrective actions my system had learned.

Adolph’s (1993) finding that crawling infants who had learned to avoid steep slopes plunged headfirst down those same slopes the moment they stood up and walked is the same phenomenon (pp. 219-220, Thelen & Smith, 1994). The knowledge was not stored as a proposition (“steep slopes are dangerous”) that could be accessed from any posture. It was a perception-action trajectory specific to the crawling state space: a particular visual angle, a particular felt resistance in the arms, a particular proprioceptive configuration of prone balance. Standing up relocated the system to a distant region of the state space with no established connection to the crawling-based attractor. My upright cycling attractor, built through years of experience, occupied a specific region of the perception-action state space defined by an upright trunk, overhead center of gravity, direct steering, and weight-shiftable balance. The recumbent placed me at a point in the state space as distant from that attractor as standing is from crawling. I needed a declining slope to get started, an external energy source to compensate for the missing low-speed balance skill, because the pedal-torque and weight-shift strategies that maintain upright balance at low speed were mechanically unavailable in the reclined position.

What interests me is that the knowledge did not transfer even partially. I did not wobble less because of my bicycle experience. I crashed as thoroughly as if I had never ridden a two-wheeled vehicle. The developmental prediction from Chapter 8 is that transfer requires sufficient exploration from the new posture to build a new attractor, and that with extended variable experience across postures and vehicles, a more general “two-wheeled balance” category could eventually form (p. 222). I did eventually manage to ride the recumbent, but only after abandoning my upright cycling strategies and, through variable and often painful exploration, discovering the recumbent-specific couplings between steering input, pedal cadence, and the narrow range of lateral corrections available from a fixed hip position.

Got Your Nose: Microgenesis of Expectation in a Four-Year-Old

Thelen and Smith reinterpret Baillargeon’s (1986) impossible-event findings by arguing that the habituation phase of the experiment does not activate pre-existing knowledge about object permanence; it creates trajectory-based expectations through time-locked perceptual activity (pp. 225-229). The “knowledge” infants demonstrate is a product of the preceding minutes of experience. Shaped by, but not reducible to, developmental history. I have a four-year-old daughter, and I have watched this process operate in real time across years of daily interaction.

When she was around two, I performed the standard “got your nose” trick: pinching her nose, pulling my hand away with my thumb protruding between my fingers. She was genuinely surprised. She checked her face with her hands. The object-permanence attractor for “my nose stays attached to my face” was shallow enough at that age, or the perceptual violation salient enough, that the trick produced authentic expectation violation. By four, the same trick is a game. She knows her nose is still there. The attractor for facial feature permanence is deep enough that no sleight of hand destabilizes it. What changed is not that she acquired a new piece of abstract knowledge (“noses are permanent”), but that two additional years of continuous perceptual experience with her own face, mirrors, and other people’s faces deepened the attractor to the point where the trick’s perceptual trajectory is captured and dismissed rather than captured and violated.

The potty-training episode is more revealing. I used chocolate coins as a reward, producing them from “behind her ear” after each successful toilet use. She was initially shocked that I could find chocolate money behind her ear. The magic trick created a perception-action category linking a specific context (successful toilet use), a specific perceptual-motor sequence (the trick), and a specific reinforcer (chocolate). This is structurally identical to Rovee-Collier’s mobile paradigm: a contingent reinforcer coupled with a specific context and a specific action creates a perception-action attractor that generates expectations and drives behavior.

What happened next illustrates the system’s capacity for generalization and its limits. She gamified the trick. She began flushing the toilet and claiming she had used it in order to trigger the reward sequence. She had abstracted the contingency (toilet flush predicts chocolate) but overgeneralized it, stripping away the component (actually using the toilet) that I considered definitional. I responded by telling her there was no coin behind her ear and that I did not know why. This was a novel violation: the expected trajectory (flush, trick, chocolate) was captured by the attractor but then departed from the projected course, exactly the structure Thelen and Smith describe for the impossible event (p. 229). The first few times this happened, she admitted she had not used the potty. The violated expectation generated surprise sufficient to reveal the actual contingency. The attractor recalibrated: not “flushing predicts chocolate” but “using the toilet, verified by a parent who somehow knows, predicts chocolate.” The category was narrowed through a perturbation that destabilized the overgeneralized basin and selected a more specific trajectory.

Moral Reasoning at the Edge of Multiple Attractors

Smith, Jones, and Landau’s (1992) finding that a one-word contextual shift (“a dax” vs. “a dax one”) bifurcates children’s categorization from shape-based to color-based attention demonstrates that small contextual forces produce qualitatively different cognitive organizations (pp. 241-243). Thelen and Smith propose, following Kelso, Scholz, and Schöner (1986), that intelligence is a system that lives on the edge of multiple attractors, able to move between organized states with small forces. The developmental trajectory is toward increasing context sensitivity, not increasing abstraction away from context.

Kohlberg’s (1981) stage theory predicts that individuals who reach post-conventional reasoning should not regress to earlier stages. The attractor, in stage-theoretic terms, is permanent once acquired. This prediction fails. In academic and institutional contexts, faculty and students routinely self-regulate their speech to fit environmental constraints. The same ethical commitment expressed freely in a private conversation is modulated or withheld in a departmental meeting, a public lecture, or a social media post. The concern is concrete: who is listening, who is recording, and whether future goals (tenure, funding, institutional standing) are contingent on what is said in the moment.

This is a system operating near the boundaries of multiple attractor basins, where small contextual perturbations (audience composition, recording status, institutional climate) shift it between qualitatively different modes of moral reasoning. A person reasoning from universal ethical principles in one context may reason from instrumental self-interest in another, not because the principled attractor has been erased, but because contextual forces push the system into a different basin. The stage model treats this as failure. The dynamic account treats it as the expected behavior of a system whose moral cognition is assembled in the here and now from the interaction of developmental history and immediate context.

The parallel to the word-learning data is structural. “A dax” produced a deep, narrow shape attractor resistant to perceptual perturbation. “A dax one” produced a bifurcation sensitive to stimulus properties (pp. 241-243). In moral reasoning, the “frame” is the perceived structure of consequences. When consequences are minimal (e.g., a private conversation), the post-conventional attractor dominates. When consequences are salient (surveilled institutional speech), the system bifurcates. The contextual force does not erase the principled attractor; it prevents the system from settling into it. The system hovers at the boundary, and the smallest additional perturbation (a recording device, an administrator in the vicinity) tips it. Kohlberg’s theory cannot account for this because it treats stages as permanent acquisitions. The NDS framework forecasts it.
