Chapter 3 intends to redefine developmental change. The move is to replace accounts in which the end-state is specified before the process begins with accounts in which form arises from ongoing interactions among components of a system maintained far from thermodynamic equilibrium. Thelen and Smith are not proposing a model of any particular phenomenon. They are establishing the criteria by which developmental explanations should be constructed and evaluated.

Replacing Teleology with Emergence

Maturationist, information-processing, and nativist frameworks share a teleological core: the end-state precedes the process. Whether the prescription resides in genes, neural programs, or innate principles, development negates the arrow of time. At best, these approaches freeze development as a series of stagelike end-states. At worst, they are tautological: organisms develop because everything is getting better.

The chapter uses the Belousov-Zhabotinskii chemical reaction as a counter-paradigm. Simple chemicals spontaneously generate elaborate spatial and temporal patterns. Nothing in the individual molecules prescribes these forms. The patterns emerge from nonlinear interactions among components within particular energetic constraints. This is the model of explanation Thelen and Smith import into developmental science: a science for systems with a history, where novelty can be created and the end-state is not coded anywhere.

Emergence here does not mean unpredictability or missing mechanism. It means that the behavior of the collective is qualitatively different from the sum of its parts, that the organization arises from interactions rather than from any component's prescription, and that specifying the interactions and constraints is what constitutes explanation. The system selects a preferred configuration out of many possible states, but behavioral variability is an essential precursor to that selection.

Self-Organization and Attractor Dynamics

Two criteria define the systems of interest: complexity (systems and subsystems interacting nonlinearly) and existence far from thermodynamic equilibrium (maintained by continuous energy flux). Systems at equilibrium have run down to maximum entropy; nothing new can emerge. Biological systems maintain and increase their order only through continuous exchange of energy and matter with the environment. They are dissipative structures.

When such systems self-organize, one or a few modes of behavior dominate. The order parameter compresses the enormous degrees of freedom of the elemental components into a describable collective variable. The system occupies an attractor: a preferred region of its state space. The potential well captures attractor stability. Very stable attractors look inevitable and are easily mistaken for products of hard-wired structures or programs. Thelen and Smith argue that many configurations in action and cognition that appear permanently programmed are stable attractors whose stability limits may be shifted under appropriate circumstances. Object permanence and walking differ from transitive inference and visual illusions not in kind but in the depth and breadth of their attractor basins. Development becomes the continual stabilization and destabilization of preferred attractor states over time.

Phase Shifts and Control Parameters

New forms arise through phase shifts produced by scaling a control parameter. Temperature changes to the BZ reaction are continuous, but the behavior of the system is discontinuous. At a critical value, the system jumps to a new attractor regime. The control parameter is entirely nonspecific to the resulting pattern. It carries no information prescribing the nature of the outcome. The pattern emerges from the nonlinear dynamics of the system.

This concept challenges prescriptive models. Horse gaits shift discontinuously from walk to trot to gallop with increasing speed. The gait at any speed is the most energy efficient. The gaits are so stable they look encoded, but they are as much a product of the energy and information flowing through the system as of the hardware. Order is not in the system; it is created in the process of the action.

Fluctuations, Stability, and the Status of Variability

Dynamic theory makes specific predictions at transition points. Coupled component subsystems produce intrinsic fluctuations. Within certain ranges of the control parameter, the system maintains its preferred pattern despite the noise. At critical points, the system loses this ability and fluctuations become enhanced. The system may display transient behavior with no stable pattern before evolving into a new attractor state.

Relative variability around a mean state becomes a tool for assessing system stability. Growing instability is detectable by increased variability; new stable patterns are marked by reduced variability. Kelso's bimanual finger experiments confirm this: fluctuations around mean phase increase before and during a transition from antiphase to in-phase coordination, then decrease after the transition. Recovery from perturbation slows as the system approaches the transition frequency (critical slowing down).

This reframes noise in experimental psychology. Variable and inconsistent subject performance that would conventionally be discarded as error becomes data indexing system dynamics. It indicates that the task does not elicit stable performance, that subjects may be in transition, or that several quasi-stable attractors exist.

Nested Timescales as Constitutive of Explanation

The chapter introduces formal relationships among timescales that transform the role of time in developmental explanation. The local relaxation time measures recovery from perturbation. The global stabilization time measures approach to globally stable states. The rate of control parameter change determines whether transitions appear as dramatic phase shifts or gradual reorganizations.

These relations determine whether change looks stagelike or continuous. If the control parameter changes slowly relative to equilibration, the system finds stability before reaching the critical point: no enhanced fluctuations, no observable transition. If the control parameter changes rapidly, the system cannot find stability before the critical point, producing dramatic disruption. Growth spurts during early infancy, rapid segregation of optic tracts at 3 to 4 months, and sharp increases in prefrontal synaptic density in the second half of the first year exemplify rapid parameter changes whose timescale relations produce observable transitions.

Time is not the dimension along which events are plotted. The timescale at which a process unfolds relative to other processes determines whether change appears continuous or discontinuous, whether variability appears as noise or as signal, and what counts as a developmental explanation. A theory that specifies only end-states and ignores the timescale relations among contributing processes does not explain development. It describes outcomes.

Chapter 3 establishes an explanatory framework: self-organization, attractor dynamics, phase transitions, control parameters, timescale relations, and the informational status of variability. The argument is that these principles apply to developing organisms not by analogy but because developing organisms are complex, dissipative systems. The chapters that follow show that this framework generates explanations, predictions, and experimental strategies that competing accounts cannot provide.
