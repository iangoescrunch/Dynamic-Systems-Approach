Chapter 3 establishes dynamic systems theory as an alternative explanatory framework for developmental change. Thelen positions this against conventional approaches that invoke teleological end-states, whether through maturational programs, innate structures, or information-processing architectures. The central theoretical move is rejecting explanations that presuppose outcomes and replacing them with accounts of how order emerges historically from component interactions.

The Belousov-Zhabotinskii Reaction as Paradigm Case

The chapter opens with the Belousov-Zhabotinskii reaction, which demonstrates pattern formation without prescription. Simple chemicals produce concentric circles, spiral waves, and period-doubling oscillations that lead to deterministic chaos. No component encodes these patterns. They emerge from far-from-equilibrium dynamics when energy flows continuously through the system.

Equilibrium chemistry, by contrast, has beginnings and end-states but no history. Time is not a measured dimension. Equations balance. Systems are linear and deterministic.

The Belousov-Zhabotinskii reaction violates these expectations. Ion concentrations oscillate rather than equilibrate. Smooth increases in reactant flow produce discontinuous bifurcations. Small impurities reset the entire sequence. The reaction exhibits history dependence, nonlinearity, and sensitivity to perturbations. Complexity emerges from simplicity, patterns form without designers, and deterministic systems appear random. Conventional cause-and-effect reasoning fails.

Complexity, Dissipative Structures, and Energy Flow

Dynamic systems that meet two criteria generate emergent order: complexity (many heterogeneous components interacting nonlinearly) and dissipative dynamics (maintained far from thermodynamic equilibrium by continuous energy flux).

Equilibrium systems maximize entropy, reaching maximum disorder and an inability to do work. Biological systems violate the second law locally. Their complexity increases during development rather than degrading. They achieve this by cycling energy: drawing high-negative-entropy energy from the environment, performing work, and dissipating high-entropy products. Prigogine and Stengers term these dissipative structures because order is maintained only through continuous energy exchange. When energy flow ceases, systems collapse toward equilibrium.

Self-Organization and Order Parameters

When sufficient energy drives complex systems far from equilibrium, spontaneous pattern formation occurs. Components previously lacking privileged relations suddenly coordinate, exhibiting spatial patterns and temporal regularities not predictable from individual elements.

Haken's laser provides the canonical example. Below threshold, atoms emit light independently, producing incoherent light. Above threshold, electrons transition cooperatively, generating coherent laser light. No "boss" issues commands. Cooperative behavior is self-organized.

Order parameters or collective variables compress the enormous degrees of freedom in the underlying components. The laser's light field coordinates billions of atomic transitions. The system becomes describable by one or few collective variables rather than all individual elements. High-dimensional systems produce behavior capturable by far fewer dimensions. Pattern emerges through cooperativity, reducing degrees of freedom while generating behavioral complexity.

Attractors, State Space, and System Stability

Dynamic systems occupy preferred regions of state space called attractors. State space represents all possible configurations. Attractors are states toward which the system gravitates, given particular conditions: point attractors (stable equilibrium), limit cycle attractors (periodic behavior), and strange attractors (deterministic chaos).

Attractor stability is visualized as potential landscapes. Deep wells represent highly stable attractors that require large perturbations to escape. Shallow wells are easily destabilized. This provides operational definitions of stability. Stable systems show low variability and rapid return to equilibrium after perturbations. Unstable systems show high variability and slow recovery. Near phase transitions, systems exhibit critical slowing down and enhanced fluctuations, signaling imminent reorganization.

Control Parameters and Phase Transitions

Control parameters are nonspecific scalar quantities that move systems between qualitatively different states without encoding the patterns themselves. Temperature drives water through phases but does not specify molecular arrangements. Treadmill speed drives quadrupeds through gaits but does not prescribe coordination.

When control parameters cross critical thresholds, systems undergo bifurcations: sudden qualitative changes where new attractors appear or existing ones disappear. Control parameters are nonspecific and contain no information about the resulting patterns. They simply move the system through regions where different collective organizations become stable.

Phase transitions reveal that continuous parametric change produces discontinuous behavioral change. This is fundamental nonlinearity. Small adjustments near critical points trigger major reorganizations. Between transitions, systems may be resilient, showing gradual parametric change without qualitative shifts.

Time Scales and Developmental Implications

Dynamic principles operate across nested time scales. Real-time assembly occurs on seconds-to-minutes. Developmental change spans months to years. Evolutionary processes operate over generations. Each exhibits self-organization, but slower scales constrain faster ones.

In development, behavior emerges from components that change asynchronously and nonlinearly across multiple scales. Visual stereopsis develops stepwise over weeks, while acuity improves gradually over months. Motor control follows different trajectories for different effectors.

Developmental change is recast as the evolution and dissolution of attractor landscapes. Stable behavioral forms are attractors. New forms emerge through phase transitions when control parameters cross critical values. Developing systems explore state space as old attractors destabilize, discovering new stable configurations.

Variability as Information

Traditional approaches treat variability as measurement error obscuring true performance. Dynamic systems theory elevates variability to a primary data predictor variable. Fluctuations reveal attractor stability. Low variability signals deep wells and strong coherence. High variability indicates shallow wells or proximity to transitions.

This reframes developmental instability. Periods of high variability are not regression or noise but windows where systems explore alternatives before stabilizing. Enhanced fluctuations predict imminent phase shifts. The toddler's variable gait signals emerging postural control systems reorganizing under new biomechanical constraints.

Resistance to perturbation provides another stability index. Systems in deep wells rapidly recover from disruption. Systems near transitions are easily pushed into different regions of state space. This explains décalage effects: minor manipulations have major impacts during transitions but not before or after.

Implications for Developmental Theory

Dynamic systems theory reconceptualizes developmental explanation. Traditional theories locate causation in privileged components: genes, neural maturation, and cognitive structures. Dynamic approaches distribute causation across heterogeneous subsystems and context. No component contains the developmental plan.

Teleology is rejected. Development does not "know" its endpoint. End-states do not propel change. Stable forms emerge from component interactions within constraints. Walking self-organizes because anatomical elements, neural capacities, motivation, gravity, and support surfaces collectively support certain coordinative patterns.

Emergence replaces prescription. Patterns contain more information than the inputs that generate them. The kick trajectory emerges from muscle activation, elastic properties, and gravity, producing spatiotemporal organization not encoded in any component. This is not vagueness but recognition that collective behavior is irreducible to components.

The framework shifts experimental questions. Rather than asking what matures or what representations are acquired, researchers ask: What are the control parameters? How do attractors evolve and dissolve? What is the attractor layout at different points? How does variability index stability?

Development becomes understanding how ordered progressions emerge from component interactions, with no component containing the plan. This requires identifying contributing subsystems, their dynamics, and how they assemble within task contexts.
