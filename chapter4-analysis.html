Chapter 4 extends dynamic systems principles from real-time action to developmental timescales. Thelen applies pattern formation concepts to infant motor development, using walking acquisition as the paradigm case. The theoretical move is demonstrating that developmental change operates through the same self-organizing processes governing momentary behavior, with slower timescale dynamics providing the context for faster ones.

Bridging Timescales: From Action to Development

Dynamic principles operate across nested temporal scales: real time (seconds), developmental time (months to years), and evolutionary time (generations). In real time, behavior assembles opportunistically from available components. Extending these principles to development requires recognizing that components themselves change as infants grow and mature.

No component contains a developmental clock. Just as kick rhythm emerges from muscle activation and limb properties without neural encoding, developmental sequences emerge from changing subsystem dynamics without genetic or maturational prescription.

Bernstein's Degrees of Freedom Problem

Bernstein identified the coordination problem: how does the nervous system control a system with enormous degrees of freedom in constantly changing force fields? Moving limbs generate inertial forces. The same muscle contraction produces different trajectories depending on context.

Bernstein's solution was synergies: functional groupings temporarily constraining degrees of freedom. The system coordinates collections as units that assemble dynamically for specific tasks. Critically, indeterminacy is a feature, not a bug. Rigid programming prevents adaptation. Greene reframed degrees of freedom as degrees of opportunity: the freedom to assemble and reassemble enables the emergence of new adaptive forms.

This extends to development. When established patterns dissolve, increased degrees of freedom enable exploration. Developmental transitions are windows where loosened constraints permit discovery of more functional solutions.

Self-Organization in Infant Kicking

Thelen's kicking research demonstrates self-organization in motor behavior. Young infants produce rhythmic, coordinated leg movements with smooth, simultaneous joint flexions and extensions. This initially appeared to reflect central pattern generator activity: neural oscillations producing alternating limb movements.

Electromyography revealed otherwise. At flexion, both flexor and extensor muscles contracted simultaneously. Net flexion resulted from stronger flexor activation. Leg extension showed minimal muscle activation. The leg reversed passively, driven by elastic energy and gravity.

The kick trajectory thus contains more information than the neural signal. Spatiotemporal organization emerges from neural activation, muscle elasticity, joint mechanics, and gravity. No component encodes the complete pattern. Cyclic rhythm arises without an explicit clock because spring oscillation frequency depends on physical properties.

Phase plane analysis reveals spring-like dynamics. The collective behavior compresses high-dimensional interactions into low-dimensional dynamics describable as a stable attractor. This attractor is soft-assembled, not hardwired. Components vary, yet the collective pattern remains stable, appearing programmed while remaining flexible.

Development as Evolving and Dissolving Attractors

Developmental change is reconceptualized as sequences of attractors with varying stability, evolving and dissolving over time. Recognizable behavioral forms represent collective variables that reside within limited regions of the state space, despite variability in initial conditions. Pattern emerges through component cooperativity, potentially dominating alternatives.

At any developmental point, behavior assembles from subsystems within contexts. Small critical component changes may trigger wide-ranging phase shifts, producing qualitatively different forms. Alternatively, collective variables may show linear progression or resilience despite subsystem changes.

This model has profound implications. Development is probabilistic state space exploration, not prescribed marching toward endpoints. Walking emerges because multiple subsystems (strength, balance, motivation, perceptual coupling) collectively support upright bipedal coordination under task constraints. The form self-organizes when these conditions align, not because a walking program matures.

Asynchronous and Nonlinear Change

Subsystems change asynchronously and nonlinearly. Asynchrony means components develop at different rates. Visual stereopsis appears stepwise while visual acuity improves gradually. Orofacial control precedes trunk control. No unified developmental clock times these changes.

Nonlinearity means change includes spurts, plateaus, and regressions. Subsystems undergo phase shifts: sudden, qualitative appearances and disappearances. Babbling onset and first steps are rapid and steplike. Postural control acquisition is protracted.

System stability depends on subsystem status when behavior assembles. Timescales are interwoven: cooperative interactions assemble and disintegrate as environments change while subsystems shift. No components are logically causal, as all determine the system's output. However, systems may be especially sensitive to certain parameters, in which small changes produce qualitatively distinct effects.

The developmentalist's task is discovering these control parameters through principled strategies: identify collective variables, map subsystem influences, and manipulate candidate parameters to observe reorganizations.

Variability as Developmental Information

Traditional approaches treat variability as measurement error. Dynamic systems theory elevates it to primary data. This transforms interpretation of developmental instability. A toddler's variable gait indicates reorganization of postural control systems, not regression. Enhanced fluctuations predict imminent phase shifts, not incompetence.

Resistance to perturbation provides a second stability index. Systems in deep wells rapidly recover. Systems near transitions are easily pushed into different state space regions. This explains décalage effects: minor manipulations have major impacts during transitions but not before or after.

Importantly, variability at transitions is not epiphenomenal. Systems in single deep wells have limited options. Loss of coherence frees components to reassemble. Enhanced variability enables exploration of richer adaptive solution landscapes. Skilled individuals' attractor layouts include multiple stability regions yet retain global flexibility and plasticity.

Control Parameters in Development

Control parameters drive phase transitions without encoding resulting patterns. They are nonspecific scalar variables moving systems through state space regions where different organizations become stable.

In Kelso's bimanual coordination studies, cycling frequency serves as a control parameter. Below the threshold, subjects maintain antiphase coordination. Above the threshold, the system switches to in-phase. Speed does not contain coordination information but drives the system across a bifurcation boundary.

Identifying developmental control parameters is challenging. Multiple candidates exist: physical growth, neural maturation, practice, motivation. The key criterion is nonspecificity: the parameter must not contain pattern information.

For infant stepping, one control parameter is leg mass. Newborns produce stepping when held upright. Around two months, stepping disappears. Traditional explanations invoked cortical inhibition. Dynamic analysis revealed that rapidly accumulating leg fat increased mass relative to muscle strength. Submerging infants in water restored stepping, as did supporting leg weight. The pattern was present but suppressed by biomechanical constraints.

This illustrates that phenomena traditionally explained by neural maturation may reflect shifting balances among heterogeneous subsystems. Apparent reflex loss is actually a control parameter crossing a threshold where coordinative patterns become unstable.

Implications for Developmental Science

Chapter 4 establishes that developmental explanation requires identifying how multiple subsystems with independent dynamics combine within task contexts. No component holds privileged causal status. Walking emerges from a confluence of anatomical elements, neural capacities, motivation, gravity, support surfaces, and parenting practices collectively supporting certain coordinative patterns.

This redefines explanation. Rather than locating the cause (gene, maturation, representation), researchers map multidimensional contributing factors and identify parameters controlling transitions between stable states.

Experiments manipulate candidate control parameters, observing whether small changes produce phase shifts. They measure variability and recovery from perturbations to index stability. They track how subsystems change asynchronously and how their confluence produces behavioral forms.

The framework predicts context sensitivity, décalage effects, and heterochrony. Behavior should be stable within but variable across contexts. The same competence should appear and disappear depending on task demands. Different domains should show independent trajectories.

Developmental order arises from component interactions, not programs. The task is to explain how ordered progressions emerge when no component contains the plan. This requires rigorous attention to subsystem dynamics, assembly conditions, and control parameters governing transitions.
