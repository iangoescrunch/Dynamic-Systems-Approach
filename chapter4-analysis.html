Chapter 4 applies the principles from Chapter 3 to a specific developmental problem: learning to walk. The contribution is not a description of locomotor development but a reshaping of developmental causation. Single-cause accounts are replaced with distributed, constraint-based causality, and this replacement reshapes what counts as a cause, how hypotheses are generated, and how experimental evidence is interpreted.

Rejecting Cartesian Dualism in Causal Explanation

The chapter opens by rejecting the assumption that dynamic principles may suffice for the body but that the brain requires different categories of explanation. This is Cartesian dualism in causal clothing. Dynamic principles apply at many levels of behavioral development, from neural embryology to cognition to social interaction. The brain is not excluded, but it is denied privileged status as the motor for developmental change. No single subsystem receives causal priority. This denial sets up the chapter's central argument: if no component is logically prior, then causal explanation must specify how interacting components, constraints, and control parameters organize behavior rather than how one component produces it.

Bernstein's Problem as a Problem of Causation

Bernstein identified the foundational issue: the CNS must translate intentions into moving limbs, but the body constitutes a complex linked system where the same muscle contraction has different consequences depending on the position, velocity, and force context of the limb. Movements are not chains of details but structures differentiated into details. Altering one element unavoidably changes others that may be far removed in space and time. The system's indeterminacy is not a problem to be overcome but the source of adaptive flexibility. Greene recast Bernstein's degrees of freedom as degrees of opportunity: the freedom to assemble and reassemble in response to changing needs is the wellspring for new movement forms. If behavior is rigidly programmed, there are no sources of change.

This reframes causation. The cause of a coordinated movement is not a neural command that specifies the output. It is the cooperative interaction of heterogeneous components within a task and energetic context. No element contains a privileged essence of the behavior.

Self-Organization in Infant Kicking: Coordination Without Prescription

Thelen and Fisher's EMG recordings of infant kicks provided the first empirical challenge to prescriptive causation. The CPG concept predicts alternation between flexor and extensor motoneurons. What they found was simultaneous contraction of both groups at flexion initiation, with little or no muscle contraction at extension initiation. The leg completed the cycle because elastic potential energy stored during flexion, combined with gravity, reversed the leg. The trajectory and timing were emergent: the on-line result of cooperative interactions among neural, skeletal, and muscular components within a gravitational field. The high dimensionality of the system condensed to produce a movement with properties of a spring.

There is no logical or empirical way to assign causal priority to either the organic components that belong to the infant or the environment in which the movements are performed. Both determine the collective features. The kicking pattern is so stable it looks hard-wired, but the stability is produced by soft, context-specific assembly of components that are free to vary. This distinction between the appearance of prescription and the reality of dynamic assembly is what the chapter's causal analysis rests on.

Variability as Theoretical Information

The chapter elevates variability from noise to signal data. Two measures index attractor stability: variability of the collective variable around its mean state, and resistance to perturbation. As the system loses stability, weaker cooperative bonds produce greater behavioral variability. At transitions, small changes in organism, task, or environment can produce profound reorganizations. This is the source of Piagetian décalage effects in developmental research.

The implication for experimental reasoning is direct. Standard variable-centered designs that average across subjects at discrete ages cannot reveal transitions or the loss of stability associated with transitions. Groups do not change; individuals do. Cross-sectional comparisons capture the boundaries of change but cannot clarify the developmental pathways. Variability between individuals provides clues to control parameters. Variability within individuals indexes attractor stability. A dynamic account replaces stagelike progressions with probabilistic and functionalist selection, and it requires longitudinal sampling at a density appropriate to the timescale of change.

The Disappearance of Stepping: Distributed Causality in Practice

The disappearance of newborn stepping at approximately 2 months illustrates why single-cause accounts fail and how distributed causality works. The traditional account attributed the disappearance to cortical inhibition of a subcortical reflex: a single neural cause producing a behavioral effect. But infants continue to produce stepping-like movements supine and underwater. The neural substrate has not regressed.

Thelen and Fisher found that rapid postnatal weight gain, primarily subcutaneous fat, created additional load on weak muscles. This load inhibited stepping only when the movement was oriented in the kinetically demanding upright posture. Fat accumulation acted as the nonspecific control parameter that shifted stepping from a stable to an unstable attractor. Infants with faster weight gain showed faster step decline. Arousal correlated with step rate (more energy overcomes the load). Submerging legs in water increased stepping. Adding weights decreased it.

The manipulations worked only because the system is assembled dynamically. The step attractor occupied a relatively shallow well; comparable weight additions would not change step kinematics in an older child with a stronger attractor. The control parameter (body composition) is nonspecific to the step pattern and nonobvious from a neural-maturationist perspective. Changes in the nervous system may reflect rather than generate changes at the periphery. This inverts the standard causal hierarchy.

Treadmill Stepping: Operationalizing the Causal Framework

The treadmill study (Thelen and Ulrich, 1991) operationalizes distributed causality as experimental strategy. It identifies a collective variable (alternating leg phasing), maps its attractor states over developmental time through longitudinal study, uses variability as an index of stability, exploits individual differences as natural experiments, introduces perturbations to test attractor strength, and identifies control parameters by mapping co-occurrence of subsystem changes with changes in the collective variable.

Alternating stepping stabilized between months 1 and 9, replacing multiple coordinative modes (single, double, parallel) with the singular state of alternation. The control parameter was identified as the flexor-extensor balance of the leg muscles. Down syndrome infants (hypotonic) showed delayed onset. Premature infants with additional extensor exposure to gravity showed improved early performance. The behavior does not either exist or not; it is probabilistically performed under defined circumstances.

The contrast with input-output models is sharp. Learning-theoretic accounts treat development as a mapping from environmental inputs to behavioral outputs, mediated by internal processing structures. The treadmill study shows that the coordinated behavior is not an output of an internal processor; it is an emergent product of the infant's anatomical structures, energetic status, and the mechanical action of the treadmill in cooperative interaction. No component contains the pattern. The pattern arises in the assembly.

The chapter concludes with the ontogenetic landscape: a series of potential wells whose depth and width change over developmental time as attractors evolve and dissolve. The traditional question (what causes the child to walk?) has no single answer. The dynamic question (what control parameters shift the system through its attractor landscape?) generates both the experimental strategy and the criteria for evaluating explanations. Enumeration of contributing variables is not explanation. Identification of the parameters that move the system through its transitions is.
