Chapter 7 translates the framework of dynamic categorization (Chapter 6) into empirical territory. Thelen and Smith assemble evidence from intermodal perception, motor development, and infant memory to support a single claim: knowledge is selected through multimodal, time-locked activity, not constructed through integrating separate representational modules. The chapter does catalogue infant abilities. It reinterprets each line of evidence as a perspective on the same selectionist process, which operates across timescales—from seconds in a habituation trial to the months over which locomotion reorganizes spatial cognition.

Sensory Integration as the Developmental Primitive

The chapter opens by reversing a primary assumption. Piaget (1952) treated the sensory modalities as initially separate systems that require progressive coordination through assimilation and accommodation. Reaching, in that account, is built from the serial linkage of grasping, sucking, and looking reflexes across five stages. The neuroanatomical evidence reviewed here contradicts this sequence. Damasio (1989) found no localized “association area” for perceptual binding; instead, divergent, convergent, and reentrant projections interconnect sensory cortices at every level. Stein and Meredith (1993) demonstrated that the superior colliculus, a phylogenetically old midbrain structure, contains neurons responsive to visual, auditory, and somatosensory input simultaneously, organized in overlapping topographic maps that constitute a “multisensory-multimotor map” (p. 115, Thelen & Smith, 1994). The enhancement effect is nonlinear: multimodal stimulation amplifies neural response most when individual signals are weak, precisely the condition under which integration is most adaptive.

Behavioral data confirm this primacy. Newborns orient visually toward heard sounds (Mendelson & Haith, 1976). By one month, infants match the oral feel and sight of textured pacifiers (Meltzoff & Borton, 1979). Four-month-olds preferentially watch visual events that actively match concurrent auditory events (Spelke, 1976). The developmental task, then, is not to construct intermodal coordination but to select from the massive space of possible multimodal associations those that correspond to persistent real-world correlations. Cross-modal experiments do not reveal a competency infants “have”; they reveal, in real time, the same selectionist process through which perception-action categories form over developmental time. The familiarization period of such experiments creates dynamic attractor basins through the time-locked activation of neuronal groups as the theory of neuronal group selection (TNGS) predicts (Edelman, 1987).

Movement as Perception

Thelen and Smith treat self-produced movement not as the output of a perceptual-motor pipeline but as a perceptual modality in its own right. In every act of looking, mouthing, or reaching, proprioceptive and kinesthetic signals enter reentrant maps time-locked with exteroceptive input. Singer’s (1990) finding that proprioception from eye muscles is necessary for binocular perception in the cat visual cortex illustrates the principle: without the motor signal, the perceptual category cannot form. If movement is perception, then strictly “within-modality” learning may not exist at all. Every visual familiarization includes synchronous eye-movement signals; every haptic exploration includes arm and finger proprioception.

This principle carries a strong developmental prediction: cognitive transitions should be paced by motor milestones, because motor skill determines the range of multimodal sampling available to the system. The evidence converges from multiple directions. Bertenthal, Campos, and Barrett (1984) showed that locomoting infants, whether crawling or using wheeled walkers, performed spatial orientation tasks that non-locomoting infants of the same age failed. Kermoian and Campos (1988) refined this finding: belly crawlers, who keep their faces close to the floor and cannot visually track landmarks, performed no better than non-locomotors. The mechanism is not mysterious; without head control sufficient to track spatial locations during self-movement, the correlated multimodal input that forms allocentric spatial categories is unavailable. Bushnell and Boudreau (1993) documented corresponding motor-perceptual dependencies in haptic development: tactile discrimination at 6 months requires the lateral rubbing movements that emerge at 4 months; configurational shape detection requires bimanual exploration, which develops late in the first year. The perceptual milestone follows the motor one because the motor pattern is the control parameter that enables the requisite multimodal sampling.

The Acredolo, Adams, and Goodwyn (1984) experiment demonstrates a link at the microgenetic timescale. Twelve-month-olds who locomoted independently to retrieve a hidden toy performed better than those carried passively, and, critically, the active infants visually tracked the target during movement, whereas the passive infants did not. By 18 months, both groups succeeded regardless of condition. The attractor for allocentric spatial coding deepened with accumulated locomotor experience; control parameter manipulation no longer destabilized the system. This is a textbook signature of attractor dynamics: during the transition from stable states, performance is sensitive to small perturbations; once the attractor deepens, the same perturbations are absorbed without disruption (pp. 197-198, Thelen & Smith, 1994).

Memory as Attractor Formation

The chapter’s most detailed empirical case comes from Rovee-Collier’s mobile conjugate reinforcement paradigm. Three-month-olds trained to kick a mobile to produce movement remember the contingency for about two weeks and forget gradually thereafter. The dynamics of this memory are neither simple nor linear. Infants trained with a distinctive crib bumper remember the mobile-kicking contingency only when tested with the same bumper after three days, although within 24 hours, the bumper change is not disruptive (Butler & Rovee-Collier, 1989). Thelen and Smith model this as interacting attractor basins: the mobile, being highly salient and providing rich reentrant input through contingent kicking, forms a deep basin; the distinctive bumper forms a shallower, associated basin. While the mobile basin is strong (within 24 hours), activation from the mobile cue alone is sufficient to recruit the motor memory. As the basin flattens over time, the nearby context basin becomes part of the retrieval path. Change either component, and the weakened network cannot recruit the full perception-action category.

The generalization data provide the critical test. Infants trained on a single mobile for two sessions did not respond to a novel mobile 24 hours later. Infants trained on a different mobile each session generalized to a novel mobile the next day (Fagen, Morrongiello, Rovee-Collier, & Gekoski, 1984). Variable training created overlapping attractor basins with shared features, producing a wider basin accessible from multiple entry points. Identical training produced a steep, narrow basin that rejected novel stimuli. This is not a learning curve; it is a selectionist mechanism. TNGS predicts that degenerate, overlapping neuronal groups will be selectively strengthened when time-locked inputs provide “multiple takes” on the same category. The mobile studies demonstrate this process at the timescale of days. The developmental implication is that the same process, operating over months and years of variable experience, produces the broad, generalized knowledge structures that Piaget attributed to stage-like reorganization.

Context Sensitivity as Developmental Expansion

Six-month-olds show stronger context effects than three-month-olds. Reminders are effective only when the training context matches (Boller et al., 1990; Borovsky & Rovee-Collier, 1990). This looks counterintuitive if memory is a storage capacity that increases with age. It is entirely predictable if memory is attractor formed by the encoding field. Borovsky and Rovee-Collier (1990) propose that infants’ “progressive expansion of the range of focal attention” (p. 1580) indicates that, at 6 months, more contextual features are encoded in the perception-action category. The attractor is not weaker; it is richer, incorporating distal context that was previously ignored. This expansion coincides with the onset of independent locomotion, which itself requires landmarks and spatial surrounds. The 6-month-old is encoding the training environment because the motor system is approaching a state in which that information will become functionally relevant.

The chapter bridges real-time dynamic learning and developmental change without calling on separate mechanisms. The same process of time-locked reentrant mapping that produces a context-specific kicking memory in three minutes produces allocentric spatial cognition over three months. Décalage across domains is expected, not anomalous, because the control parameters that enable multimodal sampling develop heterochronically. Vision, haptics, locomotion, and manual exploration each contribute disjunctive input at different rates to create the characteristic pattern of asynchronous competencies that structuralist accounts label as stage transitions. In the dynamic account, there are no stages. There are attractors forming, deepening, and broadening as the developing system explores the space of possible perception-action couplings through its own variable activity.
