Chapter 5 adds neural mechanism to the abstract dynamic principles established in preceding chapters. Freeman, Georgopoulos, Merzenich, and Edelman each contribute a piece: chaos as source of novel forms, population coding of movement, experience-driven cortical reorganization, and selection from degenerate neural repertoires. Each mechanism finds concrete expression behind a drum kit.

Chaos and the Source of Novel Forms

Freeman's central claim is that chaotic neural dynamics provide the substrate for novel activity patterns. Perception is not the copying of a stimulus but a transition between chaotic attractor basins, and the diversity of chaotic activity provides the "trials" of trial-and-error learning. Improvisation on a drum kit instantiates this process. When I improvise a fill, I do not plan it as an assembly of component strikes. My hands and feet generate a sequence that I anticipate will sound good, based on accumulated experience and feedback. The system produces candidate trajectories from within its attractor landscape; experience-driven selection has shaped which trajectories are retained. This parallels Freeman's account exactly: chaos provides structured variability, not randomness, and the organism selects from that variability through repeated cycles of action and consequence.

The developmental parallel is direct. Many mistakes precede confident improvisation, just as infant reaching begins with variable, poorly targeted movements. Those errors are the exploratory variability that a selectionist system requires. Without them, no selection occurs.

At very high tempos, improvisation becomes impossible. The cognitive and physical load is too high to deviate from the established pattern; I can elaborate with rolls and cymbal crashes but not create novel sequences. This reveals the boundary condition on chaos as resource: when the system is fully committed to maintaining a complex coordinative structure at its performance limit, no capacity remains for exploratory variability. The attractor is deep but narrow.

Population Coding and the Paradiddle as Collective Variable

Georgopoulos demonstrated that movement direction emerges from the vector sum of a population of broadly tuned cortical neurons, not from any single cell's command. A paradiddle (RLRR LRLL) provides a concrete behavioral analog. At slow tempos, I experience each stroke as a discrete, consciously directed action. As tempo increases, the pattern undergoes a qualitative shift: four individual commands become a single gestalt, a coordinative structure assembled from the collective dynamics of both hands. The paradiddle ceases to be a sequence and becomes a shape.

This transition has a critical boundary. When tempo exceeds the system's capacity to sustain the RLRR LRLL pattern, the hands switch uncontrollably to simple alternation: LRLRLR. The system does not stop. It falls into a simpler, more stable attractor to preserve global coordination. Before the full transition, there is a brief zone of flickering between patterns (LRLLLRLLLRLLRLRLRLRL); the alternation is not a regression to a previous rhythm but a rapid, stochastic exploration before the system commits to the simpler coordinative structure. This flickering maps onto the enhanced fluctuations at critical points that Thelen identifies as signatures of phase transitions in Chapters 3 and 4, and that Freeman's neural dynamics grounds in the instability of competing attractor basins.

Stopping is never acceptable in performance. This task constraint forces the system to find some coordinative solution rather than none, just as the infant who cannot yet walk fluently recruits whatever combination of crawling, cruising, or supported stepping the current state allows.

Hysteresis and the Rebound Transition

The paradiddle also demonstrates hysteresis. As I warm up and increase tempo, there is a specific zone where the pattern locks in and becomes effortless. This lock-in coincides with a biomechanical phase shift: at sufficient speed, strikes become rebounds. My fingers work the back end of the sticks on a pivot at the front of my grip, creating a lever system where the double bounce is a product of sustained finger pressure rather than discrete wrist thrusts. The effort shifts into something like Morse code (. . - . . - . . -), alternating hands, controlling rebounds rather than generating each individual strike.

The lock-in tempo and the breakdown tempo are not the same. The pattern can be sustained at speeds above the lock-in point but breaks down at a higher threshold, and the breakdown is sudden rather than gradual. This gap between lock-in and collapse is hysteresis: the system's behavior depends on its history, on whether tempo is increasing or decreasing. Thelen discusses hysteresis as a signature prediction of dynamic systems applied to development. Here it manifests within a single practice session, confirming the continuity between real-time and developmental timescales that the book insists upon.

Cortical Plasticity and the Structure of Decay

Merzenich showed that cortical maps enlarge with repeated stimulation and revert when training stops. Drumming follows a parallel trajectory. With sustained practice, the tempo at which the conscious paradiddle shifts into automatic motion increases; the attractor deepens. Without practice, precision degrades first. The beat sounds sloppy, out of sync, but not necessarily out of time.

The order of degradation reveals the skill's state space structure. Spatial accuracy degrades before temporal accuracy. At high speeds, the stick tip must travel feet in a fraction of a second to reach the next component. Spatial targeting fails first; temporal synchronization degrades second, with attacks between limbs drifting by fractions of a second, audible to observers and felt through kinetic feedback by the player. The deepest dimension, the last to decay, is basic alternation; at its core, four-limb coordination is as fundamental as walking.

For an experienced player, return to previous ability is rapid. Practice reactivates and re-deepens attractors that have shallowed but not dissolved. This suggests that extensively trained cortical maps retain structural traces even when the functional map has contracted.

Degeneracy and Reentrant Mapping

Edelman's theory requires degeneracy: multiple neural configurations producing the same functional output. Playing drums tests this directly. I can play with my eyes closed. I can play with my ears silenced. But I cannot play without proprioceptive and haptic feedback; I need to feel the stick strike the surface, estimate its position, and judge the distance for the next attack.

This hierarchy reveals which reentrant mappings are load-bearing. Vision and audition are degenerate channels whose information can be compensated by other modalities. Proprioception and haptic feedback are non-negotiable. The degeneracy is asymmetric: some channels carry more coordinative load than others.

The reentrant coupling between auditory and motor systems is perhaps most striking. When I hear a complex rhythm, my hands and feet reproduce it almost instantly without verbal description. The motor and auditory systems are coupled through reentrant connections that generate a perception-action category directly. Sheet music transports rhythmic information across distance and time; it does not mediate between perception and performance. Many virtuoso drummers never learn to read notation.

Air drumming illustrates the depth of this coupling. I can see my kit with my eyes closed and execute patterns on an invisible instrument because thousands of hours of multimodal coupling have internalized the spatial-temporal structure. Kit customization matters because each drummer's configuration becomes part of their attractor landscape. Classical setups enforcing uniformity assume the instrument is a fixed structure to which the player adapts. This reflects the same homogeneous logic Thelen challenges in stage theories. The instrument-player system is the unit of analysis.
