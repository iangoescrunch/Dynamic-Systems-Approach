Chapter 10 represents the culmination of the book’s empirical argument. Thelen and Smith take the A-not-B error, one of the most studied and theorized phenomena in developmental psychology, and reframe it entirely within their dynamic systems framework. The A-not-B error is the well-known finding that infants around eight to ten months of age, after successfully retrieving an object hidden at location A on several trials, continue to reach toward A even after watching the object being hidden at location B. For Piaget, this perseverative error was diagnostic of incomplete object permanence: the infant had not yet fully differentiated the object from the action used to retrieve it. The error’s disappearance around twelve months marked the consolidation of Stage IV sensorimotor intelligence.

The Challenge to Piaget

Thelen and Smith do not merely critique Piaget’s interpretation. They dismantle the entire framing. Their argument is that the A-not-B error is not about what the infant knows or does not know about objects. It is about the real-time dynamics of a coupled perception-action-memory system operating under specific task constraints. The infant does not fail a conceptual test. The infant’s reaching behavior is a trajectory through a dynamic field in which prior motor history, visual input, postural context, and memory decay interact to produce perseveration under some conditions and correct search under others.

The Coupled Systems Model

The chapter develops a formal model of the A-not-B task as a dynamic field. The critical insight is that the infant’s reach decision is not a discrete computation that occurs before movement initiation. Instead, it is a continuous process in which an activation field evolves over time under the influence of multiple inputs. The field represents a spatial dimension (the A-B continuum) and the activation at each point in this space reflects the combined influence of specific input (the current hiding event at B), nonspecific input (general arousal, postural readiness), memory input (the lingering trace of previous reaches to A), and the intrinsic dynamics of the field itself (cooperativity among nearby sites, inhibition between distant sites).

On A trials, all inputs converge: the object is hidden at A, the infant reaches to A, and the memory trace at A is strengthened. By the time the object is hidden at B, the memory trace at A is a strong, self-sustaining peak in the activation field. The specific input now favors B, but it must compete against the accumulated memory at A. Whether the infant reaches correctly to B or perseverates to A depends on the relative strength of these competing inputs, and that relative strength depends on task parameters: the delay between hiding and reaching, the salience of the hiding event, the number of prior A trials, and the infant’s postural state.

Context-Dependence as the Central Prediction

The power of the dynamic field model is that it generates a unified account of the many contextual factors known to influence perseveration. This is precisely where Piaget’s account fails and where nativist accounts cannot even engage. If the A-not-B error reflects a stage of cognitive development, then it should be robust across contextual variation. It is not. Increasing the delay between hiding and search increases perseveration, because the specific input from the B-hiding event decays while the memory at A remains. Reducing the number of A trials reduces perseveration, because the memory peak at A is weaker. Changing the infant’s posture between the A and B trials can eliminate perseveration entirely, because the postural change disrupts the motor planning component of the memory trace.

This last finding is particularly devastating to competence-based accounts. If the error reflects a conceptual limitation, what the infant understands about objects, then changing the infant’s posture should be irrelevant. Bodies do not think about objects. But in the dynamic field model, posture is not peripheral. The memory trace is not an abstract representation of “object at A.” It is an integrated perception-action-memory state that includes the specific postural configuration from which previous reaches were launched. Disrupt the posture, and you disrupt the motor planning field, weakening the perseverative attractor and allowing the specific input from B to dominate.

The Crawling Connection

Thelen and Smith connect the A-not-B phenomenon to locomotor development in a move that ties the chapter back to the book’s opening arguments. Infants who have begun crawling show different patterns of perseveration than pre-crawling infants of the same age. This is not because crawling teaches infants about object permanence in any direct way. It is because crawling changes the infant’s relationship to spatial layout, provides new proprioceptive and vestibular inputs during search, and alters the motor possibilities available during the task. The crawling infant approaches the A and B locations from different postural configurations, and these different configurations produce different dynamic fields with different attractor structures. The connection also illustrates the book’s broader point about coupled timescales: real-time trial dynamics (seconds), learning across repeated trials (minutes), and ontogenetic changes in body proportion and locomotor experience (months) are nested processes whose interaction at a particular developmental moment determines whether memory and motor history dominate over current perceptual input.

Dissolving the Construct

The chapter’s deepest contribution is not the dynamic field model itself, which would be further formalized by Spencer and colleagues in subsequent years, but the argument that “object permanence” is not a coherent construct. Piaget treated the A-not-B error, object search in general, and the infant’s understanding of object persistence as expressions of a single underlying competence that develops in stages. Thelen and Smith argue that there is no single competence. There is a family of task-specific behavioral patterns, each assembled in real time from the available perception-action-memory resources, each sensitive to its own set of contextual parameters, and each following its own developmental trajectory. What looks like a stage transition, the disappearance of perseveration around twelve months, is actually the convergence of several independent developments: stronger specific input processing, more robust working memory, changing body proportions that alter the motor planning field, and accumulated experience with search tasks that biases the system toward attending to current hiding location.

This is the decoupling move that was previewed in Chapter 2’s critique of Piaget and that the intervening chapters on locomotion, reaching, and categorization have prepared the reader to accept. Developmental psychology has organized itself around constructs, object permanence, conservation, theory of mind, executive function, that are assumed to be unitary competencies developing on coherent timetables. Thelen and Smith’s analysis of A-not-B suggests that these constructs may be convenient descriptive labels for families of context-dependent behaviors that share no common underlying mechanism. The implications extend far beyond infant cognition. Any domain in which psychologists invoke a unitary construct to explain a family of related but context-sensitive behaviors is a domain where the dynamic systems framework would ask whether the construct is doing explanatory work or merely providing a name for the phenomenon it claims to explain.
