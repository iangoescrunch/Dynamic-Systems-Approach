Chapter 1 established that locomotor development resists a single-cause explanation. Chapter 2 extends this to cognition, presenting identical puzzles: competence coexisting with incompetence, capacities present in some contexts but absent in others, and asynchronous trajectories. Thelen examines how contemporary cognitive theories fail to account for developmental data.

Cognitive Development's Paradoxes

Research on transitive inference illustrates the paradoxical nature of cognitive development. Trabasso demonstrated that preschoolers succeed at transitive inference (A > B, B > C, therefore A > C) when the premises are thoroughly drilled and phrased bidirectionally. With such support, preschoolers infer as adults do.

This creates problems parallel to treadmill stepping. Preschoolers make transitive inferences with support but systematically misremember comparative premises as categorical properties. They seriate mentally but not physically. They demonstrate logical competence under specific conditions yet fail in everyday contexts, as Piaget described. How can cognition be simultaneously competent and incompetent?

The data compels recognition that no single competence suffices. Transitive inference requires understanding comparative terms, conceptualizing dimensions as continuous, remembering relations, and deploying procedures. These develop asynchronously and context-sensitively. The puzzle is explaining both continuities and discontinuities within a unified framework.

Competence Versus Performance

The competence-performance distinction emerged to reconcile early abilities with typical failures. Performance imperfectly reflects competence because immature memory, attention, and language limit expression. Stripping away performance demands revealed precocious competencies.

This derives from Chomsky's linguistic competence model. Competence is formal, abstract knowledge; performance involves its deployment through general mechanisms and neural hardware.

However, the distinction is problematic. Theoretically, it commits to single causation. But no competence alone suffices. Language developmentalists cite conceptual limitations; concept developmentalists cite linguistic limitations. Real cognition requires the simultaneous engagement of concepts, language, memory, and attention.

Empirically, competence cannot be directly assessed. The distinction also lacks biological plausibility. If competence is genetic, how is formal structure instantiated in neural processes?

The distinction makes sense only if cognition divides into independent levels. But if systems continuously interact, the distinction dissolves. Development is about how pieces combine.

Nativism and Innate Constraints

Demonstrations of early competence inspire nativist proposals: cognition reflects innate designs limiting possible knowledge structures. The nativist argument builds on Quine's indeterminacy of translation. When someone says "rabbit," it could mean hopping, white, furry, or rabbit parts. Yet language learning succeeds, suggesting hard-wired constraints.

Spelke's falling objects research illustrates this. Four-month-olds find violations of physical expectations surprising. Since infants make sophisticated inductions rather than simpler ones, beliefs must be innate.

This commits two errors. First, it assumes cognition consists of represented beliefs acquired through induction. When induction fails, nativists conclude that no development occurs rather than questioning whether cognition might be something else. Second, it denies the empirical fact of development. Despite continuities, infants and adults differ fundamentally.

What does "innate" mean biologically? Nativists propose abstract blueprints. These are vacuous. Biologically plausible accounts involve the joint guidance of initial structure, the activities it engenders, and typical developmental environments. Chick pecking develops from passive neck bending, as heartbeats move the head. Mealworm recognition develops from seeing one's own feet.

Newport's findings are revealing. People who learned second language early show greater syntactic sensitivity than later learners. Why should cognitively immature children learn deeper regularities? Newport speculates cognitive immaturity helps. Young children process fragments; adults process everything. Deep properties are more transparent in fragments. Language may exploit cognitive immaturity rather than requiring blueprints.

Modularity

Fodor's modularity hypothesis proposes cognition comprises autonomous, informationally encapsulated modules accessing limited information, operating rapidly but inflexibly. The Müller-Lyer illusion persists despite knowledge, suggesting visual perception is encapsulated.

Developmental evidence shows heterochrony; systems develop asynchronously. But modularity raises unanswered questions. First, where are the boundaries? Language understanding combines encapsulated subsystems with interactive mechanisms. Visual experience combines encapsulated devices with general knowledge. If real cognition combines both, we must explain integration.

Second, where do modules originate? Adult architecture need not match infant architecture; modularity may be a developmental product. The Stroop effect is informationally encapsulated but emerges through learning. Syntax may be bootstrapped from semantics. Evidence concerns adult vision and language, irrelevant to developmental questions.

Third, how do modules relate? They must fit coherently. Giraffe necks require special cardiovascular support. This necessitates developmental interactions between systems and real-time interactions for coherent behavior. Modules cannot be completely encapsulated in dynamic systems.

Across evolutionary time, one species' change affects others. Across developmental time, change in one mental module necessitates change in others. Order and new forms emerge from complex interactions among heterogeneous forces. The power source lies not in separate modules but in their mutual interactions.

Information Processing and Connectionism

Information processing attributes development to general-purpose mechanisms and knowledge acquisition. Components (encoding, retrieval, attention, strategies) derive from chronometric adult cognition studies. Research isolates components responsible for age differences.

The approach reached an impasse. Everything matters and everything develops. Success in any task depends on encoding, memory, attention, strategies, and knowledge simultaneously. This seamless interaction makes isolating causal components impossible.

Connectionism is promising because it attempts synthesis, explaining how global states emerge from local interactions. However, connectionist models fail as developmental theories through four failures. First, they ignore brain's heterogeneous structures, building cognition from homogeneous nodes. Second, they solve toy single-task problems rather than explaining simultaneous diverse tasks. Third, they focus on end-states rather than developmental trajectories. Fourth, they answer old questions without recognizing that the questions are wrong.

Traditional psychology explains stability; why we mean the same by "cat." Connectionism shows how stable patterns emerge from network activity. But the central problem is not stability but change: how minds change, where new knowledge comes from, how organisms adapt. A dynamic systems account makes no use of representations. The yardstick is not rule-like behavior but rather the explanation of developmental change itself.

Teleology and the End-State Focus

Traditional theory focuses on accurate representations; connectionism focuses on asymptotic activity. This end-state focus characterizes developmental theorizing. Development is movement toward a goal, measured by distance from that goal. The danger is teleology infiltrating mechanisms. In nativist approaches, the end-state becomes the mechanism: written into the organism, propelling development. In connectionist models, the end-state directs teaching. The end-state evaluates progress in information processing; children somehow know they do not know enough.

By placing the end-state within the mechanism, we presuppose what we are explaining. Development does not "know" where it is going. There are no constraints on acting like levies. There is no set end-state other than life's end. Development is the outcome of self-organizing processes within continually active living systems. The crisis in cognitive development lies in the emphasis on constancy and the failure to study change processes. The task is to construct a theory that explains how ordered progressions emerge from component interactions, where no component contains the plan.
