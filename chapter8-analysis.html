Chapter 8 advances the central argument of Part III: that knowledge originates in, and remains constituted by, the real-time details of perceiving and acting within distinct contexts. Where Chapter 7 established selectionism as the mechanism through which perception-action categories form, Chapter 8 specifies the causal role of context. Context does not trigger pre-existing knowledge; it makes, selects, and adapts knowledge. This claim carries direct consequences for experimental reasoning. If knowledge is always of a piece with the here and now, then the standard research strategy of stripping away contextual variation from emergent competence misidentifies the phenomenon.

Global Structure and Local Variability Are the Same Thing

The chapter opens by distinguishing two perspectives on behavior. At low magnification, behavior shows global structure: cats walk with alternating limbs, reaches converge on targets, the word “mother” carries a recognizable meaning. At high magnification, behavior is variable and situation-dependent: cat walks require different muscle patterns on flat ground versus slopes, each reach follows a unique kinematic path, “mother” means different things in different sentences. Traditional explanatory strategies assume a constant underlying structure (a CPG, a motor plan, an essence) that produces global regularity, then treat local variability as performance noise. But a constant structure that outputs a fixed pattern cannot explain how the same global form is realized through fundamentally different local details across contexts (p. 216, Thelen & Smith, 1994). Something else is doing the work.

The dynamic alternative dissolves the distinction. Global order is the history of perceiving and acting in defined contexts. Context plays three causal roles: it makes the global order through repeated here-and-now experiences, it selects which global order is expressed, and it adapts that order to the existing task. These are not additive contributions but the constitutive dynamics of the system.

Slopes and the Posture-Specificity of Knowledge

Adolph, Eppler, and Gibson (1993a,b) provide the chapter’s most striking empirical case. Crawling 8.5-month-olds and walking 14-month-olds were tested on adjustable slopes. Both groups attempted ascent regardless of steepness, since falling forward while climbing has mild consequences. On descent, the groups diverged. Walkers hesitated, explored, and adapted their locomotion as slope increased; crawlers fell down steep slopes to near-certain falls. This is not because crawlers lack knowledge of heights. The same crawlers who flung themselves down 40-degree slopes avoided the visual cliff without hesitation. Crawlers could also detect slopes perceptually; they hesitated more on steeper grades. What they lacked was the ability to match their crawling abilities to the biomechanical demands of slope descent.

Longitudinal data clarify this. Adolph (1993) followed infants from early crawling to walking. New crawlers plunged recklessly; with experience, attempts aligned to slope steepness. Upon walking, ten of fifteen infants once again plunged down steep slopes. In a single session, an infant could hesitate as a crawler but immediately walk over the edge. Knowledge was posture-specific, with the “too steep” category as a crawling state-space attractor. Standing repositioned the system because upright and prone locomotion share few associations. Generality results from extended variable experience, not initial conditions.

Reinterpreting “Impossible” Events

The chapter then reconsiders Baillargeon’s (1986) finding that infants look longer at impossible events (a cart passing through a solid box) than possible events (a cart rolling behind a box placed off the track). Baillargeon concluded that infants possess abstract knowledge of object persistence, solidity, and continuity. Thelen and Smith offer a process-level alternative. They propose that during the habituation phase, coupled “what” and “where” systems trace a movement through an internal state space. The what system registers object identity (screen, cart, box); the where system registers spatial location of movement. Through repeated habituating trials, this coupled trajectory becomes an attractor functioning as a set of expectations about what happens next at each point in the sequence.

The critical analysis concerns where the test-event trajectories fall relative to the habituating attractor. The possible event begins with a novel element (the box behind the track) that diverges from the habituating trajectory, but once the screen is replaced and the cart rolls, the trajectory falls directly onto the attractor: mildly novel at start, then predictable. The impossible event differs structurally. When the box is revealed on the track, the initial what-where activity falls near the end of the habituating trajectory. The attractor captures the activity and projects it forward. But subsequent sensory input (the cart rolling through the box) departs from the projected course. Surprise follows from violated trajectory-based expectations, not from violated abstract knowledge of object persistence.

This process-level account generates falsifiable predictions that the nativist account does not. For example, changes in infant posture between habituation and test, or 90-degree rotations of the event display, should disrupt the trajectory overlap and eliminate the differential looking. Changes in static object properties like cart color should matter less, since they do not substantially alter the what-where trajectory (pp. 229-230, Thelen & Smith, 1994). The empirical strategy is reversed: rather than designing experiments to strip away context and reveal competence, the researcher systematically perturbs context to map the attractor landscape.

Novel Words and the Bifurcation of Attention

Smith, Jones, and Landau’s (1992) studies of novel word interpretation provide the chapter’s clearest demonstration of context as a causal force producing qualitatively different behavioral organization. Three-year-olds shown a novel object and told “this is a dax” attended to shape when choosing other “daxes.” This noun-shape bias was a deep, narrow attractor; virtually all children converged on shape regardless of stimulus properties, even when the exemplar was covered in glittering material. But when the same children were told “this is a dax one” (adjective frame), attention bifurcated. With matte stimuli, the adjective pulled attention toward shape. With glittering stimuli, the adjective pulled attention toward color. This is a nonlinear interaction between syntactic frame and stimulus salience, producing a bifurcation: from a single attractor basin (noun-shape) to two distinct basins (adjective-shape and adjective-color) separated by a small contextual perturbation.

Smith (1992) developed a performance-area algorithm plotting individual children’s yes/no judgments in a state space defined by shape-matching and color-matching responses. The size of the performance area indexes the depth and width of the attractor. For count nouns, the performance area is small and tightly concentrated in the shape region. For adjectives with glitter stimuli, the performance area shifts to the color region. The variability between children in each condition is not noise; it is a direct measure of the attractor landscape’s topology. Narrow performance areas indicate deep attractors. Broad, scattered areas indicate shallow or competing attractors during a transition period.

The developmental data mirror the chapter’s logic. Early word learners form a broad shape attractor. With experience in varied contexts, multiple attractors emerge, so small contextual shifts (noun vs. adjective, object features) move the system between qualitatively different states. Intelligence, as Kelso et al. (1986) propose, is living on the edge of multiple attractors, shifting states with small forces. A system locked in one attractor is rigid; one with none is chaotic. The developing child exists between these extremes, where both history and present context jointly shape knowledge, expectation, and action.
