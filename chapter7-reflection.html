The Crash Box and the Limits of Explanation

I learned to drive in a 1959 Volkswagen Beetle with a crash box first gear that required double-clutching to synchronize. The car had been passed through three generations of cousins as a trainer for farming equipment. Someone explained the procedure to me: clutch in, shift to neutral, clutch out, match the engine speed, clutch in, shift to first. The explanation was useless. Not incomplete or unclear, but categorically unable to produce the perception-action coupling that double-clutching requires. The synchronization point is the convergence of auditory input (engine pitch), proprioceptive feedback (the lever’s resistance dissolving at the match point), and kinesthetic timing (the speed of the foot on the clutch pedal). These signals are time-locked in the act of doing. No description of them generates the category, because the category is the coupled activation itself, not a proposition about when to move your foot.

Thelen and Smith’s claim that movement functions as a perceptual modality (pp. 193-194) reframes this experience. The kinesthetic and proprioceptive signals from the clutch foot and shift hand were not feedback confirming a motor plan; they were perceptual input, entering reentrant maps alongside the engine’s auditory pitch and the felt vibration of the drivetrain. The “feel” of synchronization was a dynamic category selected through repeated, variable attempts. My early efforts were noisy, with stalls, grinding, and over-revving that provided disjunctive sampling of the state space. Through that variability, the system identified the narrow corridor where the signals cohered. The corridor became an attractor. Singer’s (1990) finding that proprioception from eye muscles is necessary for binocular coherence in the cat visual cortex is the same principle operating in a different modality: without the motor signal, the perceptual category cannot form.

This extends to every mechanical and trade skill I have taught myself. Changing oil on a car, soldering electronics, building a fire, using a heat gun or torch: in each case, I read the manual and watched a video, and in each case, encoding did not begin until I performed the task. The first oil change took two hours and required the construction of a tool to reach a component. The second took a fraction of the time. By the third or fourth repetition, waiting for the oil to drain took longer than my procedure. Reading and watching produced a declarative scaffold, but the perception-action category that constitutes the skill formed only in the act of doing. Bushnell and Boudreau’s (1993) argument applies: the motor pattern is the control parameter that enables the requisite multimodal sampling. Until the hands are on the drain plug, there is no proprioceptive-haptic-visual coupling to select.

Motorcycles and the Reorganization of Perception

Bertenthal, Campos, and Barrett (1984) demonstrated that self-locomotion functions as a control parameter for spatial cognition: crawling infants succeed at spatial tasks that age-matched non-crawlers fail. The mechanism is not maturation but the multimodal information that self-movement generates. I have an adult analog. Learning to ride a motorcycle reorganized how I perceive the geometry and physics of driving, retroactively and across vehicles.

On a motorcycle, looking through turns is not a matter of style; it is a biomechanical necessity. The rider’s head and eye position control the body’s center of gravity, which in turn controls the bike’s steering. Where you look is where you go. This motor-perceptual coupling forced me to develop a specific pattern of looking: fixating on my desired point of existence several seconds into the future rather than tracking the road surface immediately ahead. Target fixation illustrates the coupling in its failure mode. If I fixate on an obstacle, my hands steer toward it. If I fixate on a painted line, I lose my spatial reference for avoiding objects and navigating hazards. The motor component (gaze direction) and the perceptual category (the geometry of the trajectory) are not separable. When the motor component is captured by the wrong target, the perceptual category collapses.

The developmental consequence was that I became a substantially better car driver. My awareness of other cars and agents in the traffic system expanded. Their velocities, trajectories, and probable future positions were incorporated into a continuous prediction and error-minimization cycle that operated farther into the future and across more variables than before. I began cornering a car using the full road rather than following a painted line, entering turns at higher velocity, and using outside-inside-outside line geometry to preserve momentum rather than scrubbing it through lateral resistance. The motorcycle did not add a new skill alongside my existing driving category. It replaced the perceptual substrate. This is what the chapter means when it argues that motor milestones act as control parameters for cognitive transitions (p. 195): the new motor skill opens access to multimodal information that was always available but never sampled, and the resulting perceptual categories retroactively reorganize performance in the prior domain.

Variable Training and the Breadth of the Attractor

Rovee-Collier’s mobile paradigm demonstrates that infants trained on a single mobile do not generalize to a novel mobile, whereas those trained on a different mobile each session do (Fagen et al., 1984). Variable training creates overlapping attractor basins with shared features; identical training creates a steep, narrow basin that rejects unfamiliar stimuli. I spent several years as a valet driver, parking hundreds of cars: trucks with low gears, Ferraris with stiff clutches and tight ratios, and once, an old first-generation Beetle identical to the one I learned on. The Beetle’s perception-action category reactivated immediately. The double-clutch feel, the engine pitch, the long throw of the shift lever: the car itself was a sufficient retrieval cue to pull up the full motor memory, even years after my last drive. This is Rovee-Collier’s reminder effect: the original training context reactivates the attractor without requiring relearning (pp. 206-207).

The Ferraris and heavy trucks were different. I stalled them in front of customers more than once before learning the nuances of their clutch engagement points and gear ratios. But I learned them quickly, within a few trials rather than the hours the Beetle originally required. The valet experience had created a broad “manual transmission” category through varied training across vehicles. Each new car activated shared features of the category (clutch pedal, shift lever, engine-load coupling) while requiring local adaptation to the specific vehicle. The basin was broad enough to capture a Ferrari but shallow enough at the edges that the first attempt did not produce a clean launch. With a few repetitions, the vehicle-specific parameters deepened into a local attractor nested within the general category. This is the selectionist mechanism the chapter describes: degenerate, overlapping neuronal groups strengthened by time-locked input from multiple takes on the same functional task (p. 209). I could still drive a Ferrari F355 today, not because the motor memory is “stored” somewhere, but because the attractor basin formed through that variable experience is broad and deep enough to be reactivated by the contextual cues of any car with a gated manual shifter.

Frequency, Delay, and the Transition Between Attractors

When I drove in England, I never reached for the shift lever with the wrong hand. Inside the car, visual cues specified the layout unambiguously: the lever was where my left hand could reach it. What failed, repeatedly, was approaching the car from the outside. The object representation “car” activated my old attractor for car entry, and I walked to the left side, sometimes opening the door and sitting down before discovering that the steering wheel had, to my momentary shock, relocated. My travel companions made the same error. The perception-action category “approach and enter my car” was an attractor formed through thousands of repetitions of a specific trajectory: approach from a specific side, reach for a specific door handle, sit in a specific seat. The English car presented identical visual features (shape, color, door handles) that captured the old attractor before the contextual correction (right-hand drive) could intervene.

The transition dynamics followed the pattern the chapter predicts for the plateau between stable attractors (pp. 197-198). When I entered and exited the car three to five or more times per day over multiple days, I began approaching from the correct side. If I left the car parked for several days and returned, the error recurred. The new attractor was shallow, frequency-dependent, and delay-sensitive. Long gaps between trials allowed the old, deeper attractor to recapture the approach trajectory. This is the same dynamic Rovee-Collier documented in infant memory: the time-dependent weakening of recently formed attractor basins, with the old, better-established basin re-emerging as the dominant retrieval path when the interval between activations is too long.
