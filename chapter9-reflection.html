Countersteering and the Conversion of Attractors

Gabriel's problem was that his natural movement created a repeating back-and-forth flapping, like a child waving both arms. To reach, he needed to change this pattern into a movement that would stop near the toy. He did this by making his arm stiff, which reduced the flapping and let him move toward the toy slowly and carefully. No one told him to do this. He found this method by changing the only thing he could control: how stiff his arm was.

Countersteering on a motorcycle is the same conversion. At speed, a motorcycle in a straight line occupies a stable attractor maintained by gyroscopic forces on the wheels and the geometry of the steering head. To initiate a turn, the rider pushes the handlebars in the direction opposite the intended turn. This is counterintuitive and, for new riders, terrifying, because it deliberately destabilizes the system. The push causes the front wheel to track laterally, the bike's center of gravity shifts past the contact patch, and the motorcycle falls into the lean angle that produces the turn. The rider has disrupted a cycle attractor (stable upright travel) to create a new attractor (the cornering arc) whose radius and stability depend on lean angle, throttle, and body position.

Gabriel stiffened to stop his arm shaking. A motorcycle rider does the opposite at entry: the initial push makes the bike unstable. But both actions perform the same function. They scale a control parameter (stiffness in Gabriel's case, steering torque in the rider's) to move the system from one attractor regime to another. Once the bike is in the lean, the rider modulates body position, throttle, and rear brake to control the resulting trajectory, just as Gabriel modulated co-contraction to guide his hand toward the toy. Neither the infant nor the rider plans the trajectory at the level of kinematics. Both set initial dynamic conditions and let the system's physics determine the path.

The Hannah contrast extends the analogy. At low speed, a motorcycle's gyroscopic stability weakens. The straight-line attractor becomes shallow. Riders compensate by counter-leaning: shifting body weight in the opposite direction of the turn to keep the combined center of gravity over the contact patch. This is a force-generation problem, not a force-damping problem, structurally identical to Hannah's task of lifting her low-energy arms against gravity (p. 260). The same vehicle, the same rider, presents fundamentally different control problems at different speeds. The intrinsic dynamics of the system at the moment of action determine what the rider must solve, just as Gabriel's and Hannah's temperamental and biomechanical starting conditions determined what each infant had to solve.

New riders who have not yet discovered countersteering attempt to turn by leaning their bodies or turning the handlebars as they would a bicycle. These strategies, imported from a prior attractor landscape, produce either no turn or a loss of control. The motorcycle requires a solution that cannot be transferred from bicycles, explained verbally, or represented before it is performed. The rider discovers the coupling between handlebar input, lean angle, and turn radius through variable exploration. The first successful countersteer feels accidental. With repetition, the perception-action category stabilizes until perturbations (crosswinds, road camber) are absorbed without conscious correction.

Drumming and the Multi-System Assembly

Chapter 9 argues that reaching is not directed by a single plan. Instead, it is built from components working together in the moment, where no component contains a plan for the behavior (p. 263). Playing drums is a good test of this claim because it involves much more movement degrees of freedom than reaching with one arm.

Consider a basic rock pattern: right hand on the hi-hat at eighth notes, left hand on the snare at beats two and four, right foot on the bass drum at beats one and three, left foot holding the hi-hat clutch closed. Four limbs, four targets, four rhythmic subdivisions, all coordinated into a single output that sounds like one instrument. No limb contains the pattern. The bass drum foot knows nothing about the snare hand's timing. The pattern is the collective variable that condenses the system's degrees of freedom into a coherent coordinative structure.

The intrinsic dynamics matter. My right hand's resting state produces a faster, looser motion than my left. When I learned the hi-hat/snare split, the asymmetry created a Gabriel-like problem on the left side (insufficient speed and force for clean backbeats) and a Hannah-like problem on the right (excessive energy that overdrove the hi-hat). I solved them as the infants did: by modulating stiffness independently in each limb. The left wrist stiffened to produce sharper, more forceful strokes. The right wrist loosened, letting the stick rebound from the hi-hat surface as opposed to driving through it. These were not instructed adjustments. They were discovered through weeks of variable, often poor attempts, during which the system explored force-timing combinations until functional patterns stabilized.

Adding the kick drum introduced a postural control parameter that parallels Chapter 9's finding that Nathan's postural milestones co-varied with reaching phases (p. 276). The right leg, now committed to the bass drum, could no longer stabilize the torso. My trunk had to be reorganized to maintain balance on the remaining three contact points (seat, left foot, and the dynamic shifting of weight across the pelvis). This postural reorganization did not feel like a reaching problem, but it constrained reaching in the same way that sitting, crawling, and walking constrained Nathan's arm control. Until trunk stability was solved, limb coordination suffered. The system-wide parameter (postural control) had to be in place before the local parameters (limb-specific stiffness and timing) could be refined.

Valet Driving and the Breadth of the Attractor

Nathan's year-long reaching data show a trajectory of exploration, plateau, and stabilization that does not follow a linear improvement curve (pp. 272-273). The variability during the first months is not noise. It is the system sampling a wide region of force-speed space before selecting efficient solutions. Thelen notes that by the end of the first year, the attractor for reaching is deep enough that perturbations that once destabilized performance are absorbed: infants reach from varied postures, to varied locations, with varied objects (p. 276).

My valet experience followed the same trajectory, compressed over a shorter timescale. Each novel vehicle presented unfamiliar clutch engagement points, gear ratios, throttle responses, and seating positions. I stalled in front of customers. I ground gears. Some vehicles had clutch pedals so stiff that my left leg fatigued; others had engagement points so high that my ankle ran out of travel. Each failure constituted a data point in a high-dimensional phase space defined by pedal weight, engagement height, throttle sensitivity, and shift-lever throw.

What changed over months was not an accumulation of vehicle-specific memories. The system did not store a lookup table mapping each car model to its clutch parameters. What stabilized was a general perception-action category for manual transmission launching: a broad attractor basin that captured the shared dynamics across all vehicles. The collective variable was the felt match between engine load (auditory and vibratory) and clutch release rate (proprioceptive). This coupling condensed the high-dimensional space into a single perceptual index that transferred to novel vehicles on first contact. A car I had never driven would stall me once, sometimes not at all, because the general attractor was broad enough to capture the new vehicle's dynamics and deep enough that the first few seconds of clutch-feel exploration were sufficient to locate the vehicle-specific parameters within the basin.

This is what Thelen means when she writes that infants form "a category of appropriate stiffness controls that could transport their hands to the toy from a variety of starting positions" (p. 269). The category is not a representation of reaching stored in memory. It is a functional basin in the perception-action state space, assembled through variable experience, that captures novel instances because their dynamics fall within the basin's boundaries. My manual-transmission category and Nathan's reaching category were both products of the same process: extended variable exploration that selected, from a high-dimensional space, the subset of perception-action couplings that solved the task across contexts.
