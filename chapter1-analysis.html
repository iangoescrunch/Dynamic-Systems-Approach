What does it mean to say an organism "develops"? At low magnification, development appears orderly, progressive, and directional. Infants proceed through recognizable sequences with reliable timing. This regularity invites a teleological explanation; development appears to be guided by design. The central problem is identifying where instructions reside and how they generate behavioral change.

Traditional answers locate instructions in genetic blueprints (maturational accounts) or environmental structure (learning accounts). Interactionist positions propose gene-environment interactions. All three share a logical flaw: they assume preexisting structure explains emergent structure, producing infinite regress. If mental structure comes from neural structure, and neural structure from genetic code, what governs translation from one-dimensional sequence to three-dimensional architecture? This requires additional instructions, generating explanatory regress.

Chapter 1 resolves this through empirical confrontation. Thelen examines locomotor development, demonstrating that traditional frameworks like neural maturation, central pattern generators, cognitive control; fail to account for developmental data.

The Puzzle of Walking: Orderliness at Low Magnification

McGraw and Gesell cataloged motor development into sequential stages, proposing cortical maturation drives progression. McGraw's seven phases of erect locomotion exemplify this: reflex stepping gives way to cortical inhibition, producing a static phase, followed by deliberate stepping as cortical participation increases, culminating in integrated walking. The cortex, initially unmyelinated, progressively inhibits subcortical patterns while assuming neuromuscular control.

This neural-maturational account aligns with observable regularities. Newborn behavior appears reflexive, consistent with subcortical dominance. As cortical areas differentiate, behavior becomes variable and intentional. Corticospinal tract myelination coincides temporally with walking onset. The account is parsimonious: cortical maturation explains a universal sequence.

Contemporary accounts share this single-cause logic. Forssberg proposed newborn stepping manifests a phylogenetically ancient spinal pattern generator, later replaced by a human-specific pattern. Zelazo invoked higher-order cognitive influences converting reflexive patterns into instrumental actions. Despite theoretical differences, these accounts assume development proceeds through predetermined stages driven by a privileged mechanism.

Confrontation with Data

Empirical scrutiny dissolves apparent unity. Newborn stepping and supine kicking, treated as distinct in stage accounts, are kinematically identical: simultaneous flexion and extension of the hip, knee, and ankle, with similar timing and muscle activation. They differ only in posture.

This creates explanatory problems. Stepping "disappears" around 2 months upright while kicking persists throughout the first year supine. If cortical inhibition suppresses subcortical stepping, why only upright movements? If disuse causes loss, why do identical movements persist in different contexts?

Context manipulations challenge unitary causation. Stepping rate correlates with arousal and increases when infants are submerged. Three-month-olds who do not step on surfaces step vigorously when submerged. Weights suppress stepping. Practice prevents decline. These manipulations shift trajectories through mechanisms that are orthogonal to cortical maturation.

The treadmill phenomenon is revealing. Seven-month-olds who do not step autonomously produce coordinated alternating steps on motorized treadmills. They adjust step rate to belt speed and maintain alternation, making asymmetrical adjustments to each leg while preserving coordination. This appears involuntary yet adaptive.

Longitudinal treadmill data show that coordinated stepping emerges by 3-4 months, improves through 6 months, then plateaus; this profile differs from autonomous stepping. Treadmill stepping is kinematically more mature than newborn stepping or early deliberate steps. Some infants exhibit heel strike on treadmills, previously considered definitive of mature locomotion.

Decomposing Unitary Behaviors

These findings demonstrate that walking decomposes into distinct components with asynchronous developmental courses. Weight support, alternating leg movements, and intentional control develop at different rates and respond to different contextual factors. Treadmill stepping exposes leg-patterning mechanisms divorced from both weight support and intentional control. Neuromuscular mechanisms for coordinated alternation function months before infants can stand or step autonomously.

Context dependency is equally fundamental. Leg movement patterns have no context-independent essence. The same substrate produces stepping, kicking, or no movement depending on posture, arousal, and task. There is no essence of walking in motor cortex or spinal cord; the treadmill elicits more locomotor-like behavior than any neural structure studied in isolation.

Theoretical Implications: Single-Cause Models Fail

Neural-maturational accounts fail empirically. They predict uniform trajectories driven by cortical maturation, but the data show context-dependent, manipulable developmental trajectories. Weight gain predicts stepping decline. Water and weights bidirectionally modulate stepping. Practice prevents decline. Treadmills reveal patterns months before they appear spontaneously. These phenomena are incompatible with the development of a neural plan unfolding.

Central pattern generator accounts fare no better. Isolated spinal cords in cats produce rhythmic output, interpreted as hardwired locomotor programs. However, cats with only spinal output cannot walk functionally; they cannot navigate terrain or adjust gait. The CPG, divorced from proprioceptive input and peripheral mechanics, is an impoverished abstraction. Functional walking requires continuous integration of neural output with limb mechanics, gravitational forces, and task demands. It emerges from heterarchical coordination among neural, mechanical, and sensory components, not hierarchical control by central programs.

Cognitive accounts invoking intentionality fail similarly. Intentional control develops context-specifically, not as a unitary capacity. Infants voluntarily activate mobiles with legs at 3 months but cannot voluntarily step on treadmills until 8-9 months. Intentionality is decomposable and task-dependent.

Goals for Developmental Theory

The empirical confrontation establishes requirements for adequate explanation. Theory must account for the origins of novelty without assuming preexisting structure. It must reconcile global regularities: universal sequences, reliable timing, with local variability: context dependency, heterochrony, decomposability. Explanation must integrate multiple levels (neural, biomechanical, behavioral) without reductionism, demonstrating how local processes generate global outcomes.

Theory must explain continuity across time scales. Real-time behavior (e.g., how infants step in contexts) must be linked to developmental time (e.g., how capacity emerges over months). Principles that explain moment-to-moment organization should also explain month-to-month progression.

Traditional frameworks—stage theories, maturational accounts, and cognitive models—fail to meet these requirements. They assume development unfolds from programs, whether genetic, neural, or cognitive. They partition contributors into privileged (internal, biological, primary) and supportive (external, environmental, secondary). They treat variability as noise rather than information about processes.

Walking puzzles, stepping disappearance and reappearance, context sensitivity, heterochrony, and decomposability are not anomalies requiring minor adjustments. They signal processes that self-organize through ongoing interactions among heterogeneous components rather than unfolding according to predetermined instructions.

The locomotor case generalizes. Similar puzzles characterize cognitive development: behaviors that appear, disappear, and reappear; capacities present in some contexts but absent in others; and the asynchronous development of related abilities. If walking resists single-cause explanation, cognitive development will prove equally resistant.

Chapter 1 establishes both empirical foundation and the theoretical stakes. Development requires explanation, but adequate explanation cannot locate instructions in any single mechanism. The task is to construct an alternative framework that explains how ordered developmental progressions emerge from component interactions, in which no component contains the plan.
